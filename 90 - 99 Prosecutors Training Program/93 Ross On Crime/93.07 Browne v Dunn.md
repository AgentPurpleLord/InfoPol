# The Case
Browne v Dunn (1893) 6 R 67
- Damages for libel, meaning a published false statement that damaged a persons reputation.
- The defendant was a solicitor.

# The Rule
>[!INFO] Bulstrode v Trimble [1970] VR 840
>"A typical formulation is that set out in _Cross on Evidence_, 3rd ed, pp 211, 212, which is in the following terms: In the cross-examination of a witness “any matter upon which it is proposed to contradict the evidence-in-chief given by the witness must _normally_ be put to him so that he may have an opportunity of explaining the contradiction, and failure to do this _may_ be held to imply acceptance of the evidence-in-chief” (italics are mine)."

The gist being:
- A witness gives his evidence-in-chief.
- During cross examination, if the cross-examining party puts evidence to them that contradicts their evidence-in-chief, the witness MUST understand that it contradicts it and they must be given a chance to explain it.
