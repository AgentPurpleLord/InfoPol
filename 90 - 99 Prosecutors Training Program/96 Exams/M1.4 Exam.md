
# Question 1
**The accused has plead guilty to a single charge of theft of motor vehicle. The magistrate has imposed a fine of $2,000 with conviction. The magistrate asks you, as the prosecutor, whether the court can do anything about the accused’s licence (which is current). What is your response?**

****What is your submission to the court? Refer to specific legislative provisions in your answer.** **Answer MUST be in IRAC format.****

**Issue**
Can the court suspend or cancel the accused's drivers licence when he is found guilty of theft of motor vehicle?

**Rule**
SENTENCING ACT 1991 - SECT 89 - Suspension or cancellation of driver licence or learner permit and driver disqualification—certain motor vehicle offences
(4) If a person is found guilty or convicted of stealing or attempting to steal a motor vehicle, the court may (in the case of a finding of guilt) and must (in the case of a conviction)—
    (a)     if the person is the holder of a driver licence or learner permit—
            (i)     suspend that licence or permit for the period of time that the court specifies; or
			(ii)     cancel that licence or permit and disqualify him or her from obtaining a further one for the period of time that the court specifies; or
(5)     If a period of suspension or disqualification is not specified by the court on making an order under subsection (4)(a), (b) or (c), the period is 3 months.

CRIMES ACT 1958 - SECT 74 Theft
CRIMES ACT 1958 - SECT 73 Further explanation of theft

**Application**
s. 74 & s. 73 Crimes Act 1958: In this case, the accused was found guilty of a single charge of theft of motor vehicle. He was fined with conviction.

s. 89(4) Sentencing Act 1991: In this case, the court **must** (in the case of conviction) either suspend the accused's licence for a period of time specified by the court; or cancel/disqualify the accused from obtaining a further one for a period specified by the court.

s. 89(5) Sentencing Act 1991: In this case, if the court does not specify a period for the suspension or cancellation of the accused's licence, the period is 3 months. 

**Conclusion**
Prosecutions submits that Your Honour must suspend or cancel and disqualify the accused's licence for a minimum period of 3 months, or a duration specified by the court.

# Question 2
Defence are objecting to a Victim Impact Statement (VIS) being tendered by the prosecutor after a plea of guilty as it contains irrelevant information. The Magistrate looks at the VIS and asks what power they have, to admit the VIS if there are inadmissible elements in it.

**What is your submission to the court? Refer to specific legislative provisions in your answer.** **Answer MUST be in IRAC format.**

**Issue**
What power does the Court have to admit a Victim Impact Statement if certain parts of it are inadmissible?

**Rule**
SENTENCING ACT 1991 - SECT 8K Victim may make victim impact statement
SENTENCING ACT 1991 - SECT 8L Contents of victim impact statement
    (3)     The court may rule as inadmissible the whole or any part of a victim impact statement, including the whole or any part of a medical report attached to it.
      (4)     It is the intention of Parliament that in interpreting and applying this section, courts have regard to the following—
        (a)     the victim impact statement allows the victim to tell the court about the impact of the offence on the victim;
        (b)     the victim impact statement is not inadmissible merely because it contains subjective or emotive material.
    (5)     The court may receive the whole of a victim impact statement despite—
        (a)     an objection being taken to the statement or part of the statement; or
        (b)     the statement containing inadmissible material.
    (6)     If the court receives a victim impact statement that contains inadmissible material, the court, in sentencing the offender—
        (a)     is not to rely on the material that the court considers to be inadmissible; and
        (b)     need not specify which of the material is not being relied on.

**Application**
Your Honour, Section 8K of the Sentencing Act 1991 provides that a victim may make a victim impact statement if a person is found guilty of an offence, for the purpose of assisting the court in sentencing.

Section 8L of the Sentencing Act 1991 outlines the contents of a victim impact statement and relevantly provides:
- At subsection (3), that the court may rule as inadmissible, the whole or any part of a victim impact statement, including the whole or any part of a medical report attached to it; and
- At subsection (5), that the court may receive the whole of the victim impact statement despite an objection being taken to the statement or part of the statement; or the statement containing inadmissible material; and
- At subsection (6), that if the court receives a victim impact statement that contains inadmissible material, the court, in sentencing the offender, is not to rely on the material that the court considers to be inadmissible; and need not specify which material is not being relied upon.

Most important, at subsection (4), "it is the intention of Parliament that in interpreting and applying this section, courts have regard to the following- the victim impact statement allows the victim to tell the court about the impact of the offence on the victim; and the victim impact statement is not inadmissible merely because it contains subjective or emotive material."

Prosecutions submission is that it is a matter for the Court to determine what is admissible or not. Prosecutions also submit the importance of allowing a victim to share their story and feel heard, which is ultimately the driving purpose behind victim impact statements.

**Conclusion**
The Magistrates' Court has a power pursuant to Section 8L(5) and 8L(6) of the Sentencing Act 1991, to accept the VIS even with inadmissible material and to rely upon / not rely upon certain material within it as it deems fit. 


# Question 3
**The accused has plead guilty to two counts of sexual assault. During the defence plea in mitigation, defence urge the court that as the accused will be placed on the Sex Offender Register the court should impose a less severe sentence as a result. The magistrate asks you, the prosecutor, what relevance the Sex Offender Register has on the determination of appropriate sentencing disposition. How do you respond?**

****What is your submission to the court? Refer to specific legislative provisions in your answer.** **Answer MUST be in IRAC format.****

**Issue**
Is the Court allowed to consider the possible registration of the offender onto the SOR during sentencing?

**Rule**
Section 5(2BC) of the Sentencing Act 1991
In sentencing an offender a court must not have regard to any consequences that may arise under the Sex Offenders Registration Act 2004 or the Worker Screening Act 2020 from the imposition of the sentence.


**Application**
Your Honour, pursuant to Section 5(2BC) of the Sentencing Act 1991, the Court **must not** have regard to any consequences that may arise under the Sex Offenders Registration Act 2004 from the imposition of the sentence. 

**Conclusion**
The Act is very clear that Your Honour must not have any regard to the Sex Offenders Registration Act in the sentencing disposition.


# Question 4
**After obtaining a pre-sentence report which assessed the accused as being unsuitable for a Community Corrections Order (CCO), the magistrate sentenced the accused to undergo a 30-month CCO with the sole special condition that he performs 300 hours of unpaid community work on charge 1 and a fine of $1,500 on charge 2.**

**Is this sentence open to the court?**

**Issue**
Is it suitable for the Magistrate to impose a sentence of a community corrections order when a pre-sentence report is *unsuitable*?

**Rule**
SENTENCING ACT 1991 - SECT 8A Court may order pre-sentence report
    (1)     If a court finds a person guilty of an offence, before passing sentence the court may order a pre‑sentence report in respect of the offender and adjourn the proceeding to enable the report to be prepared.
    (2)     A court must order a pre-sentence report if it is considering making a community correction order, a youth justice centre order or a youth residential centre order so that it may—
        (a)     establish the person's suitability for the order being considered; and
        (b)     establish that any necessary facilities exist; and
        (c)     if the order being considered is a community correction order, gain advice concerning the most appropriate condition or conditions to be attached to the order.
    (3)     A court is not required to order a pre-sentence report under subsection (2) if it is considering making a community correction order with an unpaid community work condition of up to a maximum of 300 hours as the sole condition attached to the order.
    (4)     If a court orders a pre-sentence report, it must be prepared by—
        (a)     the Secretary to the Department of Human Services if the court is considering making a youth justice centre order or a youth residential centre order; or
        (b)     the Secretary in any other case.
    (5)     The author of a pre-sentence report must conduct any investigation that he or she thinks appropriate or that is directed by the court.


SENTENCING ACT 1991 - SECT 37 Community correction order
Subject to any specific provision relating to the offence, a court may make a community correction order in respect of an offender if—
        (a)     the offender has been convicted or found guilty of an offence punishable by more than 5 penalty units; and
        (b)     the court has received a pre-sentence report (if required) and has had regard to any recommendations, information or matters identified in the report; and
        (c)     the offender consents to the order.

SENTENCING ACT 1991 - SECT 40 Community correction order may cover multiple offences
    (1)     If an offender is convicted or found guilty by a court of two or more offences, which are founded on the same facts or form or are part of a series of offences of the same or a similar character, the court may make one community correction order in respect of those offences in place of separate orders in respect of all or any two or more of them.
    (2)     A community correction order that is being made in respect of more than one offence must not exceed the maximum period for which a community correction order may be made under section 38.

SENTENCING ACT 1991 - SECT 38 Period and commencement of a community correction order
    (1)     The period of a community correction order is the period determined by the court which must not exceed
        (a)     in the case of an order made by the Magistrates' Court—
              (i)     in respect of one offence, 2 years; or
              (ii)     in respect of 2 offences, 4 years; or
              (iii)     in respect of 3 or more offences, 5 years; or
        (b)     in the case of an order made by the County Court or the Supreme Court in respect of one, or more than one, offence, 5 years.
    (2)     Unless section 44(3) applies, a community correction order must commence on a date specified by the court that is not later than 3 months after the making of the order.

SENTENCING ACT 1991 - SECT 43 Fine and a community correction order
A court may impose on an offender a fine authorised by law in addition to making a community correction order.

SENTENCING ACT 1991 - SECT 48C Unpaid community work condition
    (1)     A court which is making a community correction order may attach a condition requiring an offender to perform unpaid community work.
    (2)     The purpose for attaching an unpaid community work condition is to adequately punish the offender in the community.
    (3)     Subject to section 48CA, the offender must perform the number of hours of unpaid community work specified by the court under an unpaid community work condition.
    (3A)     If the Secretary gives a direction under section 83AU the offender must perform the number of hours of unpaid community work specified by the Secretary.
    (4)     The total number of hours for which an offender may be required to perform unpaid community work under an unpaid community work condition must be determined by the court and must not exceed 600 hours.
    (5)     The total number of hours of unpaid community work that the offender must perform in any 7 day period must not exceed 20.
    (6)     An offender may perform unpaid community work for up to 40 hours in a period of 7 days if he or she requests to do so and signs a written consent to performing the extra number of hours.
    (7)     If a court attaches an unpaid community work condition as the sole condition under this Division of a community correction order for up to a maximum of 300 hours, the order expires on the satisfactory completion of those hours of work.
    (8)     If an offender is or will be subject to more than one community correction order the court must not make a direction under this Act that causes the time limits that apply under all unpaid community work conditions under the orders to exceed the maximum time limits for the orders under section 38.
    (9)     When attaching an unpaid community work condition the court may specify that the condition applies for the period of the order or for any lesser period specified in the order.


**Application**
Section 8A of the Sentencing Act 1991 governs how the Magistrates' Court obtains a pre-sentence report, in that:
- Before passing sentence the court may order a pre-sentence report in respect of the offender and adjourn the proceeding to get that report.
- At (3), a Court is not required to order a pre-sentence report under (2) if it is considering making a CCO with an unpaid community work condition of up to a max of 300 hours as the sole conditions attached to the order.

Section 37 of the Sentencing Act 1991 provides that the Court may make a community corrections order. At (b), it provides that "the court has received a pre-sentence report (if required) and has had regard to any recommendations, information or matters identified in the report."

Nothing within Section 37 states that the Magistrate **must** adhere to an unsuitable finding on the pre-sentence report, merely that they must have regard to the recommendation that was made.

Whilst Section 38 provides that the maximum duration of a CCO would be 24 months (2 years) for a single offence, and the Magistrate here has 'separated' the two offences, Section 43 provides that the Magistrate may impose a fine and a CCO in relation to the matters.

Based upon that, I believe it follows that the Magistrates' 30-month duration is abiding by Section 38(1)(a)(ii), being that it is within the 4-year range for 2 offences that he has sentenced the accused on.

**Conclusion**
The Magistrate can impose a CCO even when the pre-sentence report indicates that the accused is *not suitable*. The Magistrate has also imposed the correct duration of the order in respect to the two offences.

# Question 5
**The accused is to be sentenced for an unlawful assault against an elderly woman who was in the local Woolworths store. The victim was unhurt but visibly shaken when police arrived. The accused has two prior matters of unlawful assault from 1 year ago, for which he received a 6 month good behaviour bond (adjourned undertaking) without conviction. The magistrate has decided to impose a fine, but seeks your assistance on whether he should impose a conviction. How do you respond?**

****What is your submission to the court? Refer to specific legislative provisions in your answer.** **Answer MUST be in IRAC format.****

**Issue**
What does the Magistrate need to consider in deciding whether to record a conviction on without conviction result in the matter?

**Rule**
SENTENCING ACT 1991 - SECT 8 Conviction or non-conviction
    (1)     In exercising its discretion whether or not to record a conviction, a court must have regard to all the circumstances of the case including—
        (a)     the nature of the offence; and
        (b)     the character and past history of the offender; and
        (c)     the impact of the recording of a conviction on the offender's economic or social well‑being or on his or her employment prospects.
    (2)     Except as otherwise provided by this or any other Act, a finding of guilt without the recording of a conviction must not be taken to be a conviction for any purpose.
    (3)     A finding of guilt without the recording of a conviction—
        (a)     does not prevent a court from making any other order that it is authorised to make in consequence of the finding by this or any other Act;
        (b)     has the same effect as if one had been recorded for the purpose of—
              (i)     appeals against sentence; or
              (ii)     proceedings for variation or contravention of sentence; or
              (iii)     proceedings against the offender for a subsequent offence; or
              (iv)     subsequent proceedings against the offender for the same offence.

**Application**
Your Honour, in considering whether to impose a conviction, or without conviction disposition, the Court should refer to Section 8 of the Sentencing Act 1991.

The court must have regard to all the circumstances of the case, including the nature of the offence, the character and past history of the offender and the impact of recording a conviction on the offenders economic or social well-being or on his/her employment prospects. 

This section also provides:
- (2) That a finding of guilt without the recording of a conviction must not be taken to be a conviction for any purpose; and
- (3)(b) A finding of guilt without the recording of conviction has the same effect as if one had been recorded for the purposes of
	- (iii) proceedings against the offender for a subsequent offence; or
	- (iv) subsequent proceedings against the offender for the same offence.

Your Honour, given that the accused has 2 previous convictions from 1 year ago of the same charge, Unlawful Assault, on which he was found guilty without conviction and given a good behaviour bond, the court is bound by Section 8(3)(b)(iv) to still consider these previous convictions in determining this matters conviction or without conviction disposition.

Prosecutions would further submit in relation to the nature of the offence:
- The victim was a vulnerable person, an elderly woman; and
- The victim whilst not being physically hurt, was visible shaken when police arrived.

**Conclusion**
The court, pursuant to Section 8, must consider the nature of the offending and previous convictions (s. 8(3)(b)(iv)) in this matter. Prosecutions submission is that the accused be found guilty with conviction.
