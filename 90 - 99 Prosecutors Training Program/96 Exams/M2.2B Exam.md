# Question 1
You are prosecuting a contested hearing of intentionally cause injury. The accused is the victim’s husband. The accused assaulted the victim by punching her in the face multiple times and squeezing her around the throat. The police were called to the house.
The victim immediately attended the police station and made a full statement to the informant. The informant has made a statement in relation to observing injuries on the victim and taking photos.
After getting independent legal advice on the day of the hearing, the victim makes an application to the court pursuant to section 18 of the _Evidence Act 2008_ to be excused from giving evidence against her husband and this application is granted.
The informant is sworn in and during his evidence, you attempt to tender the statement of the victim. Defence immediately object on the grounds the evidence is inadmissible because it is hearsay.
The magistrate asks for your response to the objection. Answer in IRAC format.

## Issue
Is the tendering of the victim's statement via the informant considered to be hearsay? Does an exception exist to allow this hearsay evidence to be admitted?

## Rule
Evidence Act 2008 Dictionary - Representation
representation includes—
(a) an express or implied representation (whether oral or in writing); or
(b) a representation to be inferred from conduct; or
(c) a representation not intended by its maker to be communicated to or seen by another person; or
(d) a representation that for any reason is not communicated;

Evidence Act 2008 Dictionary - Previous representation
*previous representation* means a representation made otherwise than in the course of giving evidence in the proceeding in which evidence of the representation is sought to be adduced;

Evidence Act 2008 Dictionary - 4 Unavailability of persons
(1)     For the purposes of this Act, a person is taken not to be available to give evidence about a fact if—
	(a)     the person is dead; or
	(b)     the person is, for any reason other than the application of section 16 (Competence and compellability—judges and jurors), not competent to give the evidence; or
	(c)     the person is mentally or physically unable to give the evidence and it is not reasonably practicable to overcome that inability; or
	(d)     it would be unlawful for the person to give the evidence; or
	(e)     a provision of this Act prohibits the evidence being given; or
	(f)     all reasonable steps have been taken, by the party seeking to prove the person is not available, to find the person or secure his or her attendance, but without success; or
	(g)     all reasonable steps have been taken, by the party seeking to prove the person is not available, to compel the person to give the evidence, but without success.

Evidence Act 2008 - Section 59 The hearsay rule—exclusion of hearsay evidence
(1) Evidence of a previous representation made by a person is not admissible to prove the existence of a fact that it can reasonably be supposed that the person intended to assert by the representation.
(2) Such a fact is in this Part referred to as an asserted fact.
(2A) For the purposes of determining under subsection (1) whether it can reasonably be supposed that the person intended to assert a particular fact by the representation, the court may have regard to the circumstances in which the representation was made.

62 Restriction to "first-hand" hearsay
(1) A reference in this Division (other than in subsection (2)) to a previous representation is a reference to a previous representation that was made by a person who had personal knowledge of an asserted fact.
(2) A person has personal knowledge of the asserted fact if his or her knowledge of the fact was, or might reasonably be supposed to have been, based on something that the person saw, heard or otherwise perceived, other than a previous representation made by another person about the fact.
(3) For the purposes of section 66A, a person has personal knowledge of the asserted fact if it is a fact about the person's health, feelings, sensations, intention, knowledge or state of mind at the time the representation referred to in that section was made.

65 Exception—criminal proceedings if maker not available
(1) This section applies in a criminal proceeding if a person who made a previous representation is not available to give evidence about an asserted fact.
(2) The hearsay rule does not apply to evidence of a previous representation that is given by a person who saw, heard or otherwise perceived the representation being made, if the representation—
(a) was made under a duty to make that representation or to make representations of that kind; or
(b) was made when or shortly after the asserted fact occurred and in circumstances that make it unlikely that the representation is a fabrication; or
(c) was made in circumstances that make it highly probable that the representation is reliable; or
(d) was—
(i) against the interests of the person who made it at the time it was made; and
(ii) made in circumstances that make it likely that the representation is reliable.
Note
Section 67 imposes notice requirements relating to this subsection.
(3) The hearsay rule does not apply to evidence of a previous representation made in the course of giving evidence in an Australian or overseas proceeding if, in that proceeding, the accused in the proceeding to which this section is being applied—
(a) cross-examined the person who made the representation about it; or
(b) had a reasonable opportunity to cross-examine the person who made the representation about it.
Note
Section 67 imposes notice requirements relating to this subsection.
(4) If there is more than one accused in the criminal proceeding, evidence of a previous representation that—
(a) is given in an Australian or overseas proceeding; and
(b) is admitted into evidence in the criminal proceeding because of subsection (3)—
cannot be used against an accused who did not cross-examine, and did not have a reasonable opportunity to cross-examine, the person about the representation.
(5) For the purposes of subsections (3) and (4), an accused is taken to have had a reasonable opportunity to cross-examine a person if the accused was not present at a time when the cross-examination of a person might have been conducted but—
(a) could reasonably have been present at that time; and
(b) if present could have cross-examined the person.
(6) Evidence of the making of a representation to which subsection (3) applies may be adduced by producing a transcript, or a recording, of the representation that is authenticated by—
(a) the person to whom, or the court or other body to which, the representation was made; or
(b) if applicable, the registrar or other proper officer of the court or other body to which the representation was made; or
(c) the person or body responsible for producing the transcript or recording.
(7) Without limiting subsection (2)(d), a representation is taken for the purposes of that subsection to be against the interests of the person who made it if it tends—
(a) to damage the person's reputation; or
(b) to show that the person has committed an offence for which the person has not been convicted; or
(c) to show that the person is liable in an action for damages.
(8) The hearsay rule does not apply to—
(a) evidence of a previous representation adduced by an accused if the evidence is given by a person who saw, heard or otherwise perceived the representation being made; or
(b) a document tendered as evidence by an accused so far as it contains a previous representation, or another representation to which it is reasonably necessary to refer in order to understand the representation.
Note
Section 67 imposes notice requirements relating to this subsection.
(9) If evidence of a previous representation about a matter has been adduced by an accused and has been admitted, the hearsay rule does not apply to evidence of another representation about the matter that—
(a) is adduced by another party; and
(b) is given by a person who saw, heard or otherwise perceived the other representation being made.
Note
Clause 4 of Part 2 of the Dictionary is about the availability of persons.


## Application
**s. 59:** In this case, the tendering of the victim's statement is considered to be hearsay. It is a previous representation made by the victim outside of court. Prosecution seek to rely upon it to assert the facts (1) laid out within it, that the victim was punched and choked by the accused. Your Honour may have regard to the circumstances of this previous representation (2A), being that immediately after the assault, the victim has reported it to police and provided a statement.

**Definition - Previous Representation:** In this case, the informant's evidence of taking the statement from the victim and therefore tendering it through the informant, is considered evidence of a previous representation. The Informant did not see, hear or otherwise perceive what occurred to the victim. The victim did experience it. She made a representation to the informant, who took a statement from her and she has signed it to agree to it being an accurate previous representation of what had occurred. 

**Definition - Representation:** In this case, the victim's experience is considered a representation. It is what occurred to her as per the facts. Her statement is a previous representation of this representation. 

**s. 62:** In this case, the informant's evidence of taking the statement is first-hand hearsay. The victim had personal knowledge of the assault that occurred to her (1 & 2). The informant does not have personal knowledge of the assault (see/hear/otherwise perceive). The informant has documented the victim's representation into a statement (previous representation).

**s. 65(1):** The accused has been charged with intentionally cause injury in this criminal proceeding. The victim is considered to be not available to give evidence of the asserted facts supporting the charge.

**Definition - Unavailable Person(1)(f) & (e):** The victim is an unavailable witness pursuant to (1)(f) & (e). Prosecution intended on calling this witness and required her to be present at today's proceedings. The victim made her own application under s. 18 which was granted (e). Prosecution can take no further reasonable steps to have the victim give her evidence about the asserted facts (f). 

**s. 65(2)(b) & (c):** The victim immediately reported the matter to police after it occurred. Her statement was taken at the time of this report. The victim had injuries and these were photographed by the informant at the time. My submission is that it is unlikely the victim fabricated the story in those circumstances and the representation made by the victim in the statement is reliable. 

Ultimately, my submission is that the statement should be admitted into evidence. There is currently no other way for the Prosecution case to proceed without it admitted into evidence, as the victim is an unavailable witness. An exception exists under s. 65(2) which would mean that the hearsay rule does not apply to this evidence. 

## Conclusion
Yes, tendering the victim's statement via the informant is considered hearsay, specifically, first-hand hearsay.
Yes, an exception exists (s. 65(2)) which means the hearsay rule does not apply to the statement.

# Question 2
The magistrate refuses defence’s objection. Defence object again on the grounds that no notice has been served by the Prosecution.

The magistrate asks for your response to the objection.


## Issue
Is notice required to be given in these circumstances?

## Rule
67 Notice to be given
(1) Sections 63(2), 64(2) and 65(2), (3) and (8) do not apply to evidence adduced by a party unless that party has given reasonable notice in writing to each other party of the party's intention to adduce the evidence.
(2) Notices given under subsection (1) are to be given in accordance with any regulations or rules of court made for the purposes of this section.
(3) The notice must state—
(a) the particular provisions of this Division on which the party intends to rely in arguing that the hearsay rule does not apply to the evidence; and
(b) if section 64(2) is such a provision—the grounds, specified in that provision, on which the party intends to rely.
(4) Despite subsection (1), if notice has not been given, the court may, on the application of a party, direct that one or more of those subsections is to apply despite the party's failure to give notice.
(5) The direction—
(a) is subject to such conditions (if any) as the court thinks fit; and
(b) in particular, may provide that, in relation to specified evidence, the subsection or subsections concerned apply with such modifications as the court specifies.

## Application
s. 67(1): In this case, the evidence was admitted via Section 65(2) as an exception to the hearsay rule. Therefore, notice must be given in these circumstances.

s. 67(4): However, I would make an application to the court to permit this evidence to be adduced without our notice obligations having been followed under s. 67(1). 

Submission: Your Honour, Prosecution had no indication that the victim was planning on making a Section 18 application until it was made. The victim has taken this step after obtaining legal advice today and was consequently excused from giving evidence against her husband.

Notice is being effectively given now, which is the earliest opportunity. While it is not in writing, the court has the discretion to alter the notice requirements in these circumstances and make a direction accordingly.

Given the critical nature of this evidence to the Prosecution case, my application is to have the notice obligations altered. This is on the basis that there is no other method of adducing the victim's evidence now. Additionally, Defence have had a copy of this statement for some time, they would not be taken by surprised having this evidence admitted. 

## Conclusion
Notice is required, however, an application can be made to the court to use its discretion on the notice requirements given the circumstances.

# Question 4
You are prosecuting a contested hearing. The accused is charged with one charge of sexual assault.
The victim wanted to hire a tradesperson to install a dog door. She contacted the accused and arranged for him to attend at her house to install the door.
The accused attended the victim’s home to install the dog door. As the victim was bending down and gesturing to the door the accused grabbed her on the bottom and said, “I’m a very skilled handyman”.
The victim told the accused to leave and called police.  The informant identified the accused via the application the victim used to hire him.  The accused has three prior convictions, one for making a false statement and two for possessing cannabis, for which he was Convicted and Fined $250.00. These convictions are from 3 years ago.
You have two witnesses: Victim and Informant. You are aware that the victim has a prior conviction for Obtain Property by Deception (OPBD) from 10 years ago for which she received an adjourned undertaking.  This has previously been disclosed to defence.
In conferencing your witness prior to the hearing, the victim tells you that she has been diagnosed with obsessive compulsive disorder and as a result remembers the incident in the laundry in significant detail.
In evidence in chief, the victim says she remembers the shorts that the accused was wearing were “navy blue with bright yellow stripes”.  You are aware that in her statement to police, she said that the accused was wearing “black shorts with bright green stripes”. 
In cross-examination by defence counsel, it is put to her that she has a prior for a dishonesty offence. It is further put to her that she is generally a dishonest person, and that she is lying to the court about what happened. 
**The Magistrate stops defence counsel and asks you for a submission. Answer in IRAC format.**

## Feedback
Nathan,
have another think about the facts of this matter as outlined in the question. In particular please look at the exception under Sec 103(1). Really think about what the prior is, when the prior was and what the disposition was. Then think about these things in the context of the charges before the court. Put simply, it is going to be a lot harder for you to make submissions/arguments per Secs 106 and 108 to re-establish the witness' credibility (the damage may already have been done). It would be better to see if there is an argument you can make around the Sec 103 exception not being satisfied by defence.
**Re-focus submission that it is not going to substantially affect the witnesses credibility.**

## Issue
Can defence introduce evidence of the victim's priors and put to the victim that she is dishonest/lying about what happened, using those priors as supporting evidence?

## Rule
101A Credibility evidence
Credibility evidence, in relation to a witness or other person, is evidence relevant to the credibility of the witness or person that—
(a) is relevant only because it affects the assessment of the credibility of the witness or person; or
(b) is relevant—
(i) because it affects the assessment of the credibility of the witness or person; and
(ii) for some other purpose for which it is not admissible, or cannot be used, because of a provision of Parts 3.2 to 3.6.

102 The credibility rule
Credibility evidence about a witness is not admissible.

103 Exception—cross-examination as to credibility
(1) The credibility rule does not apply to evidence adduced in cross-examination of a witness if the evidence could substantially affect the assessment of the credibility of the witness.
(2) Without limiting the matters to which the court may have regard for the purposes of subsection (1), it is to have regard to—
(a) whether the evidence tends to prove that the witness knowingly or recklessly made a false representation when the witness was under an obligation to tell the truth; and
(b) the period that has elapsed since the acts or events to which the evidence relates were done or occurred.

IW v The Queen [2019] NSWCCA 331 Bathurst CJ, Fullerton J, Bellew J
Paragraph 180: Firstly, it was necessary for the Crown to overcome the threshold imposed by s 103 of the Act and establish that the evidence could substantially affect the assessment of the appellant's credibility. For the purposes of s 103, the word “substantially” must be given effect. It required the Crown to establish that the line of questioning in cross-examination was capable of having a real bearing,[191](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U2&serNum=2049881135&pubNum=0006057&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&ppcid=42b47c7617cc4e2c9dd666342dbd4861&contextData=\(sc.Keycite\)&comp=wlau#co_footnote_1330205~FULLTEXT~FTNT.!191) or a real and persuasive significance,[192](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U2&serNum=2049881135&pubNum=0006057&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&ppcid=42b47c7617cc4e2c9dd666342dbd4861&contextData=\(sc.Keycite\)&comp=wlau#co_footnote_1330205~FULLTEXT~FTNT.!192) on the overall credibility of the appellant.
Paragraph 181: As I have already noted, the evidence that the appellant had previously smacked a child in his care had been removed from his record of interview because it was agreed that it was irrelevant. It could hardly be said that evidence which was irrelevant somehow had the capacity to substantially affect the assessment of the appellant's credibility so as to overcome the threshold set by s 103. Quite apart from that, the Crown conceded to the trial judge that the evidence did not advance the issue of the appellant's credibility “that far” because of the absence of any other evidence in explaining the circumstances of it. That amounted to a concession that the evidence was incapable of substantially affecting the assessment of the appellant's credibility, and that the threshold imposed by s 103(1) could not be overcome.
## Application
s. 101A: The evidence adduced and questions being asked by defence is credibility evidence. The question specifically about her criminal priors is only relevant to the assessment of her credibility. 

s. 102: Credibility evidence is not admissible without an exception.

s. 103(1): In this case, Defence are arguing that an obtain property by deception prior from 10 years ago, on which the victim was placed onto an adjourned undertaking which was complied with suggests that she has provided a false account. The wording of the act is important, it must substantially affect the assessment of the credibility of the victim.

IW v The Queen: Defence must be able to demonstrate that this line of questioning has a real bearing, or a real and persuasive significant on the victim's overall credibility. My submission would be that this line of questioning has some type of remote affect, but not a real or persuasively significant affect. It is not substantial. 

My submission is that defence do not have an exception available to them in this line of questioning and it is not admissible. 

## Conclusion
No, defence cannot introduce the victim's prior conviction as it does not substantially affect the assessment of her credibility.

# Question 5
During cross-examination, defence asked the victim about the prior inconsistent statement in relation to the colour of the shorts. Defence put to her that because she has previously made a prior inconsistent statement that the shorts were “black with bright green stripes”, she is therefore generally unreliable as a witness.  During cross examination the victim accepted that her statement said, “black shorts with bright green stripes”.  You have a copy of the informant’s notes, taken at the time she first spoke to the victim on the day of the offence where it says “dark navy shorts with bright fluoro yellow/greenish stripes”.  During re-examination, you were unable to elicit evidence of the informant’s notes from the victim. 

**You call the informant.  Can you adduce evidence of the victim’s conversation with the informant on the day of the offence and what allows you to? Answer in IRAC format**

## Issue
Can Prosecution adduce evidence of the victim's conversation via the informant?

## Rule
prior consistent statement of a witness means a previous representation that is consistent with evidence given by the witness;
prior inconsistent statement of a witness means a previous representation that is inconsistent with evidence given by the witness;

101A Credibility evidence
Credibility evidence, in relation to a witness or other person, is evidence relevant to the credibility of the witness or person that—
(a) is relevant only because it affects the assessment of the credibility of the witness or person; or
(b) is relevant—
(i) because it affects the assessment of the credibility of the witness or person; and
(ii) for some other purpose for which it is not admissible, or cannot be used, because of a provision of Parts 3.2 to 3.6.

102 The credibility rule
Credibility evidence about a witness is not admissible.

108 Exception—re-establishing credibility
(1) The credibility rule does not apply to evidence adduced in re-examination of a witness.
(2) * * * * *
Note
The Commonwealth Act previously included a subsection referring to section 105 of that Act.
(3) The credibility rule does not apply to evidence of a prior consistent statement of a witness if—
(a) evidence of a prior inconsistent statement of the witness has been admitted; or
(b) it is or will be suggested (either expressly or by implication) that evidence given by the witness has been fabricated or re-constructed (whether deliberately or otherwise) or is the result of a suggestion—
and the court gives leave to adduce the evidence of the prior consistent statement.

## Application
s. 101A: The informant's notes would be considered credibility evidence as they affect the assessment of the victim's credibility. These notes would usually be hearsay evidence as they are based on the victim's previous representation.

s. 102: Normally, this credibility evidence would be inadmissible.

s. 108(3)(a): An exception exists. Defence raised a prior inconsistent statement during the cross-examination of the victim regarding the description of the accused's shorts. This prior inconsistent statement has been admitted, making an exception to the credibility rule applicable now.

**Definition - Prior Consistent Statement:** The notes from the informant taken on the day of the offence would be classified as a prior consistent statement. These notes are consistent with her statement, which are both previous representations made in much closer proximity to the events. 

Seeking Leave: Under s. 108(3), the Prosecution must seek leave from the court to adduce this evidence. Given the circumstances, this evidence is crucial to counter the defence's implication that the victim fabricated her evidence.

## Conclusion
Yes, the Prosecution can adduce the evidence of a prior consistent statement through the informant's notes. This is done by seeking leave from the court under s. 108(3) to re-establish the victim's credibility.
