# Question 1
You are prosecuting a contested hearing for a charge of Recklessly Cause Injury.

The victim, Allen Peters gave the following evidence –
	On the 6th of January 2015, at about 11pm, Bruce Banner and the victim were having a cigarette outside the victim’s home. While they were smoking, they saw the accused in this matter, Viktor Crumb on the other side of the road.  The victim yelled out ‘look at the size of that of that guy, he is a monster’.
	The accused reacted and made a gesture with his hands across his neck, as if slitting his throat, and pointed at the victim. The victim yelled out to the accused that he better watch out or he will come over there and get him. The accused then ran across the road and punched the victim, approximately 3 times, knocking him to the ground. The victim suffered a black eye and some bruises on his face and was unable to open his eye for several days due to the swelling.
	Bruce Banner gave evidence consistent with the victim version of events.

The Police informant gave the following evidence –
	Police were called by the witness Bruce Banner to attend the scene.  When they arrived, Banner pointed out the accused and he was arrested.  The accused was interviewed and stated that he was in fear the victim and witness Banner would come across the road and get him, so he acted first.  He was afraid because there were two of them and only one of him and they said they would come and get him. 
	The accused did not give evidence at the contested hearing but relied on his record of interview. 
	The accused is 6’4” foot and 140kg. 
	The victim is 5 foot and of slim build. 

Defence make a final submission that the accused acted in self defence when he struck the victim, as he had been threatened by the victim and witness while he was walking home alone in a residential area at night.  The accused was fearful they would attack him if he didn’t act first and that his conduct was a reasonable response to the circumstances as he perceived them on the night.   

What is your submission in response?

Answer should be in IRAC form.


## Issue
Does the accused have the defence of self-defence available to him in this circumstance?

## Rule
 Crimes Act 1958 Section 322K - Self-defence
    (1)     A person is not guilty of an offence if the person carries out the conduct constituting the offence in self-defence.
    (2)     A person carries out conduct in self-defence if—
        (a)     the person believes that the conduct is necessary in self-defence; and
        (b)     the conduct is a reasonable response in the circumstances as the person perceives them.
    (3)     This section only applies in the case of murder if the person believes that the conduct is necessary to defend the person or another person from the infliction of death or really serious injury.

Notes
1     See section 322M as to belief in circumstances where family violence is alleged.
2     The circumstances in which a person may carry out conduct in self-defence include—
    •     the defence of the person or another person;
    •     the prevention or termination of the unlawful deprivation of the liberty of the person or another person;
    •     the protection of property.

Freeman v Maher [2024] VSC 682 at 19 and 25 (GORTON, J):
19: A person is not guilty of an offence if the person carries out the conduct constituting the offence in self-defence.22 In this case, the “conduct constituting the offence” was Mr Freeman's failure to undergo a preliminary oral fluid test when asked to do so and instead driving away.23 Mr Freeman acted in self-defence if he believed that his conduct was “necessary in self-defence”, and his conduct was “a reasonable response in the circumstances as he perceived them”.24 For the first limb to be satisfied, it would be sufficient if Mr Freeman genuinely held that belief, even if the belief were mistaken. For the second limb to be satisfied, Mr Freeman's refusal had to be a reasonable response in the circumstances as he perceived them, which, although based in his perceptions, is an objective test. As provided for by s 322I of the Crimes Act 1958, set out in para 10 above, if Mr Freeman led evidence that suggested a reasonable possibility of the existence of facts that, if they existed, would establish self-defence, then the prosecution had the onus of proving beyond reasonable doubt that he did not carry out that conduct in self-defence.
25: Mr Freeman's position is that it is only his own state of mind that matters when the question of self-defence arises, and that self-defence could not be disproved in the absence of direct evidence contradicting his claimed beliefs. That is not correct. Although Mr Freeman's state of mind is fundamental, a person does not act in self-defence unless, in addition to their subjective belief, the conduct is also a “reasonable response” in the circumstances as the person perceives them.26 This introduces an objective element; it is open to a court to conclude that conduct was not a reasonable response even in light of the beliefs held. Equally, and even assuming the evidentiary onus to have been discharged, the Court was entitled to be satisfied, beyond reasonable doubt, on the whole of the evidence including the video showing what Mr Freeman said and how he behaved on the day, that Mr Freeman did not in fact at the time believe that his refusal to provide the sample was “necessary to defend himself” (or his children), or that it was a “reasonable response in the circumstances” as Mr Freeman perceived them. These were matters of fact entrusted to his Honour to evaluate.27 It was well open to his Honour to form the view, in all the circumstances, that Mr Freeman was not acting in self-defence when he left the scene rather than undergo a preliminary oral fluid sample. As noted above, this is not an appeal in the nature of a rehearing or a review on the merits, and there is no basis for this Court to set his Honour's decision aside in a proceeding for judicial review. The same applies with the argument in so far as it is that Mr Freeman was acting under duress.

## Application
s. 322K: In this case, the defence of self-defence has been raised by the accused. The accused has provided that he was afraid of two individuals across the road assaulting him after they had made verbal comments leading him to believe that he might be assaulted.

The accused must satisfy the court of two elements for this defence to be complete:
- That the accused believed that his conduct was necessary in self-defence; and
- That the accused's conduct was a reasonable response as the accused perceived them.

Freeman v Maher: In this case, the accused has legitimately raised the self-defence argument. It therefore falls to Prosecution to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that this defence is not available. The first element is considered to be subjective. The second element is considered to be objective whilst considering the circumstances and beliefs of the accused.

My submission is that whilst the accused has satisfied the first element of the defence, believing his conduct was necessary, he has not satisfied the second element of the defence. This is due to the following reasons:
- The accused significantly outweighs the victim and would have a significant physical advantage in a fight; and
- The accused may have interpreted the victim's words as threats, however, the victim never made a threatening gesture, stance or moved to confront the accused; and
- The accused had alternatives available to him prior to the assault occurring. If he felt threatened, he could have called the police. He could have called out for assistance from the public. Essentially, there were other more reasonable responses that could have been attempted before a physical confrontation.

Ultimately, the Court can draw an inference from the accused's actions that he did not hold a genuine belief they would assault him. A genuine fear from a reasonable person would have resulted in different conduct. The inference drawn from the accused running over the road to confront two people that he says he was scared would assault him does not follow the accused's own logic. If he believed the basis of his fear was on being out-numbered, why run towards the group?

## Conclusion
The accused does not satisfy the second element of self-defence, being that his conduct was not a reasonable response in the circumstances, whilst considering his state of mind. 

# Question 2
You are prosecuting a contested hearing for a single charge of trafficking in a drug of dependence. The facts are that on the 26th of January 2018, the accused supplied 2 grams of heroin to an under-cover police officer. In exchange for the heroin, the accused received $200.

The accused's record of interview is tendered to the court and played. The accused tells police in interview, that he only sold the drugs because old mate Bad Boy Billy threatened to kill the accused if he didn’t start ‘slinging’ drugs for him. The accused states that the threat was made on the 24th of January 2018 at Bad Boy Billy’s home. The accused also states that he is aware that Bad Boy Billy has prior convictions for assaulting people. The accused agrees that Bad Boy Billy was not present at the scene when he was trafficking.

Interviewing police checked Bad Boy Billy on the LEAP database and found he was recorded as an associate of the accused and had four prior convictions in the past five years for assaults.  This evidence is given by the informant under cross examination.  

The accused gives evidence consistent with his record of interview. 

Defence submit that the accused only sold the drugs under duress pointing to the evidence as above.

What would you submit to the court in response to defence’s assertions in relation to the defence of duress?

Answers should be in be in IRAC form.

## Issue
In this matter, does the accused satisfy the elements of the duress defence?

## Rule
CRIMES ACT 1958 - SECT 322O
Duress
    (1)     A person is not guilty of an offence in respect of conduct carried out by the person under duress.
    (2)     A person carries out conduct under duress if—
        (a)     the person reasonably believes that—
              (i)     subject to subsection (3), a threat of harm has been made that will be carried out unless an offence is committed; and
              (ii)     carrying out the conduct is the only reasonable way that the threatened harm can be avoided; and
        (b)     the conduct is a reasonable response to the threat.
    (3)     A person does not carry out conduct under duress if the threat is made by or on behalf of a person with whom the person is voluntarily associating for the purpose of carrying out violent conduct.
    (4)     This section only applies in the case of murder if the person believes that the threat is to inflict death or really serious injury.


## Application
In this case, the accused has given evidence he was in fear of being assaulted by Bad Boy Billy and thus sold drugs under duress.

**Reasonable Belief of Threat**
s. 322O(2)(a)(i): The accused asserts he believed Bad Boy Billy would assault him unless he sold drugs. This belief satisfies the first element, as the accused's testimony indicates he feared harm from Billy.

**Only Reasonable Way to Avoid Harm**
s. 322O(2)(a)(ii): However, the second element is not satisfied. The accused had two days between the threat and his offence. The offence was not committed in the presence of Billy, which means the accused had ample opportunity to report the threat or seek a reasonable alternative to avoid harm.

**Conduct Reasonable Response**
s. 322(2)(b): The third element is also not satisfied. The accused gave evidence of being aware of Billy's priors, inferring a personal relationship with him. The accused's awareness of this fact and their possible friendship would enable an inference to be drawn that the accused may be fabricating this duress claim to avoid the consequences of the offence. 
## Conclusion
The accused does not satisfy all the requisite elements of duress.
