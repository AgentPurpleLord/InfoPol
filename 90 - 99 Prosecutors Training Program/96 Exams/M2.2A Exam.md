# Question 1
You are prosecuting a contested hearing in the Magistrates’ Court for one charge of careless driving pursuant to s 65 of the _Road Safety Act 1986_.

The accused was driving his vehicle in an erratic manner and he is alleged to have side-swiped the victim’s vehicle. 

When Police arrive, the victim told the informant that the accused had said it’s "all his fault" and then provided his details to the victim.

Prior to the hearing you are told that the victim is now deceased and had not provided a statement.

The informant says that he will give the evidence of what the victim told him at the scene.

**What do you tell Informant in relation to whether the evidence of the accused admission is admissible? Answer to be in IRAC form.**

## Issue
Can the informant give evidence of an admission that he was told by the victim, but that he did not see, hear or otherwise perceive himself?

## Rule
Evidence Act 2008 - Section 62 - Restriction to first-hand hearsay
(1) A reference in this Division (other than (2)) to a previous representation is a reference to a previous representation that was made by a person who had personal knowledge of an asserted fact.
(2) A person has personal knowledge of the asserted fact if his or her knowledge of the fact was, or might reasonably be supposed to have been, based on something that the person saw, heard or otherwise perceived, other than a previous representation made by another person about the fact.
(3) For the purposes of section 66A, a person has personal knowledge of the asserted fact if it is a fact about the person's health, feelings, sensations, intention, knowledge or state of mind at the time the representation was made.

81 Hearsay and opinion rules—exception for admissions and related representations
(1) The hearsay rule and the opinion rule do not apply to evidence of an admission.
(2) The hearsay rule and the opinion rule do not apply to evidence of a previous representation—
(a) that was made in relation to an admission at the time the admission was made, or shortly before or after that time; and
(b) to which it is reasonably necessary to refer in order to understand the admission.

82 Exclusion of evidence of admissions that is not first-hand
Section 81 does not prevent the application of the hearsay rule to evidence of an admission unless—
(a) it is given by a person who saw, heard or otherwise perceived the admission being made; or
(b) it is a document in which the admission is made.

Evidence Act Definitions
"*admission*" means a previous representation that is—
(a) made by a person who is or becomes a party to a proceeding (including an accused in a criminal proceeding); and
(b) adverse to the person's interest in the outcome of the proceeding

"previous representation" means:
1. An express or implied representation (oral/writing)
2. A representation which can be inferred from someone's conduct.
3. Representation that is not to be seen or heard by others (e.g. journal)
4. Representation that is not to be communicated (e.g. unsent letter)

**Note:** A representation otherwise made in the course of giving evidence in the proceeding in which evidence of the representation is sought to  be adduced. 

"representation" includes—
(a) an express or implied representation (whether oral or in writing); or
(b) a representation to be inferred from conduct; or
(c) a representation not intended by its maker to be communicated to or seen by another person; or
(d) a representation that for any reason is not communicated;


## Application
s. 81(1): The words used by the accused, "all my fault," would be considered to be an admission.

Evidence Act - Admission: The accused's words were a previous representation (outside of court proceeding) and they have an adverse effect on him, in that they go to proving "fault" in relation to the accident.

Evidence Act - Previous Representation: The accused's words is a representation which was made outside of the proceeding; the informant is considering adducing this evidence through himself.

Evidence Act - Representation: The accused expressed himself orally to the victim when he stated "all my fault."

s. 82: Whilst the statement "all my fault" is an admission, however, the hearsay rule still applies in this circumstance (see below at (a) and (b)).

s. 82(a): The informant is the person giving the evidence. The informant did not see, hear or otherwise perceive the admission being made. The victim is the person that did. 

s. 82(b): There is no document in which the admission was made by the accused. 

s. 62(1): In restricting the exceptions to first-hand hearsay, the informant did not have personal knowledge of the asserted fact (below). The victim had personal knowledge. 

s. 62(2): The victim had personal knowledge of the asserted fact, being that the accused stated "all my fault." 

## Conclusion
The informant's evidence would be considered an admission, but, the hearsay rule would still apply. Under that rule, the evidence is considered second-hand hearsay and no exceptions exist that would allow the evidence to be admitted.

# Question 2
During the same incident, the informant spoke to the accused who said “I don’t know what happened; I think they might have made a mistake, but I’m happy to claim it on my insurance, it’s a company car anyway”. 

When the accused produced his licence, he placed his phone down in front of the informant and the informant could see the accused’s mobile phone screen. A text message that the accused appeared to be in the process of writing was clearly visible to the informant. It said, “Crap I’ve just had an accident driving and reading your report that you needed ASAP!” It appeared the accused had not actually hit send.

During the contest you begin to lead evidence about the message the Informant saw on the accused’s phone screen. Defence object to the evidence being led on the grounds the evidence is hearsay.

## Issue
Should the informants evidence be admitted? Is it hearsay? Does an exception exist?


## Rule
Evidence Act 2008 - Section 59 The hearsay rule—exclusion of hearsay evidence
(1) Evidence of a previous representation made by a person is not admissible to prove the existence of a fact that it can reasonably be supposed that the person intended to assert by the representation.
(2) Such a fact is in this Part referred to as an asserted fact.
(2A) For the purposes of determining under subsection (1) whether it can reasonably be supposed that the person intended to assert a particular fact by the representation, the court may have regard to the circumstances in which the representation was made.

81 Hearsay and opinion rules—exception for admissions and related representations
(1) The hearsay rule and the opinion rule do not apply to evidence of an admission.
(2) The hearsay rule and the opinion rule do not apply to evidence of a previous representation—
(a) that was made in relation to an admission at the time the admission was made, or shortly before or after that time; and
(b) to which it is reasonably necessary to refer in order to understand the admission.

82 Exclusion of evidence of admissions that is not first-hand
Section 81 does not prevent the application of the hearsay rule to evidence of an admission unless—
(a) it is given by a person who saw, heard or otherwise perceived the admission being made; or
(b) it is a document in which the admission is made.

Evidence Act Definitions
"*admission*" means a previous representation that is—
(a) made by a person who is or becomes a party to a proceeding (including an accused in a criminal proceeding); and
(b) adverse to the person's interest in the outcome of the proceeding

"previous representation" means:
1. An express or implied representation (oral/writing)
2. A representation which can be inferred from someone's conduct.
3. Representation that is not to be seen or heard by others (e.g. journal)
4. Representation that is not to be communicated (e.g. unsent letter)

**Note:** A representation otherwise made in the course of giving evidence in the proceeding in which evidence of the representation is sought to  be adduced. 

"representation" includes—
(a) an express or implied representation (whether oral or in writing); or
(b) a representation to be inferred from conduct; or
(c) a representation not intended by its maker to be communicated to or seen by another person; or
(d) a representation that for any reason is not communicated;

## Application
s. 59(1): It is conceded that the informants evidence is hearsay. It is evidence of a previous representation. However, an exception applies.

Evidence Act - Previous Representation: In this case, the accused's text message would be considered a previous representation, as it was made outside of the proceeding.

Evidence Act - Representation: In this case, the informant is providing his representation of the accused's previous representation, being that he saw the text messages on the phone screen.

s. 59(2A): In this case, it is important for the court to have regard to the circumstances of the text message. It leaves the court open to draw an inference to the exact circumstances of the incident, as it outlines the accused's perspective in writing.

s. 81(1): In this case, there is an exception to the hearsay rule. The accused's text message is an admission.

s. 82: In this case, s. 82 does not apply, as the informant is giving evidence that he personally saw the text message (a). Additionally, the text message could be considered to be a document (b).

Evidence Act - Admission: The accused's text message was a previous representation (made outside the proceeding). The text message has an adverse affect on the accused due to its nature and wording. 

## Conclusion
The evidence should be admitted. It is hearsay, but, an exception exists as it is an admission. It is first-hand evidence from the informant.

# Question 3
You are prosecuting a contested hearing of one charge of recklessly cause serious injury. The accused is a male who is charged with glassing another patron at a local hotel. The accused and the victim engaged in an argument at the hotel bar. A scuffle ensued between the accused and the victim. During the scuffle the victim was hit on the forehead with a pot glass and received a serious cut.

Both the victim and his friend made statements. They both give a description of the offender as having a mohawk.

The accused was arrested at the scene and conveyed to a police station where he was photographed. He was interviewed and gave a no-comment interview however he agreed to participate in an ID parade. After the interview the informant attempted to conduct an ID parade but after trying for a month was unable to find any people of similar appearance to conduct the parade with.

The informant compiled a photo board and arranged for the victim and witness to attend to view the photo board. The photograph taken when the accused was initially arrested for this offence was used in the photo board. The photograph does not suggest that the accused was in police custody.

The photo board was shown to the witnesses 5 weeks after the offence. At the time that the victim and witness viewed the photo board the accused was again in custody, in police cells for a separate offence of burglary. He now has a shaved head. 

The witnesses viewed the photo board and both identified the accused as the assailant who had hit the victim with the pot glass.

During the contested hearing defence objects to evidence being given that the witnesses identified the accused in the photo board, claiming it is inadmissible **because** there was no identification parade held and the accused was in police custody at the time the photo board was examined.

What is your submission in response?

Answer must be in IRAC format.

## Issue
Is the identification evidence from the victim and witness admissible?

## Rule
114 Exclusion of visual identification evidence
==(1) In this section, visual identification evidence means identification evidence relating to an identification based wholly or partly on what a person saw but does not include picture identification evidence.==
(2) Visual identification evidence adduced by the prosecutor is not admissible unless—
	(a) an identification parade that included the accused was held before the identification was made; or
	==(b) it would not have been reasonable to have held such a parade; or==
	(c) the accused refused to take part in such a parade—
	==and the identification was made without the person who made it having been intentionally influenced to identify the accused.==
(3) Without limiting the matters that may be taken into account by the court in determining whether it was reasonable to hold an identification parade, it is to take into account—
	(a) the kind of offence, and the gravity of the offence, concerned; and
	(b) the importance of the evidence; and
	(c) the practicality of holding an identification parade having regard, among other things—
		(i) if the accused failed to cooperate in the conduct of the parade—to the manner and extent of, and the reason (if any) for, the failure; and
		(ii) in any case—to whether the identification was made at or about the time of the commission of the offence; and
	(d) the appropriateness of holding an identification parade having regard, among other things, to the relationship (if any) between the accused and the person who made the identification.
(4) It is presumed that it would not have been reasonable to have held an identification parade if it would have been unfair to the accused for such a parade to have been held.
(5) If—
	(a) the accused refused to take part in an identification parade unless an Australian legal practitioner acting for the accused, or another person chosen by the accused, was present while it was being held; and
	(b) there were, at the time when the parade was to have been conducted, reasonable grounds to believe that it was not reasonably practicable for such an Australian legal practitioner or person to be present—
	it is presumed that it would not have been reasonable to have held an identification parade at that time.
(6) In determining whether it was reasonable to have held an identification parade, the court is not to take into account the availability of pictures or photographs that could be used in making identifications.

115 Exclusion of evidence of identification by pictures
==(1) In this section, picture identification evidence means identification evidence relating to an identification made wholly or partly by the person who made the identification examining pictures kept for the use of police officers.==
==(2) Picture identification evidence adduced by the prosecutor is not admissible if the pictures examined suggest that they are pictures of persons in police custody.==
(3) Subject to subsection (4), picture identification evidence adduced by the prosecutor is not admissible if—
	(a) when the pictures were examined, the accused was in the custody of a police officer of the police force investigating the commission of the offence with which the accused has been charged; and
	(b) the picture of the accused that was examined was made before the accused was taken into that police custody.
(4) Subsection (3) does not apply if—
	(a) the appearance of the accused had changed significantly between the time when the offence was committed and the time when the accused was taken into that custody; or
	(b) it was not reasonably practicable to make a picture of the accused after the accused was taken into that custody.
==(5) Picture identification evidence adduced by the prosecutor is not admissible if, when the pictures were examined, the accused was in the custody of a police officer of the police force investigating the commission of the offence with which the accused has been charged, unless—==
	(a) the accused refused to take part in an identification parade; or
	(b) the appearance of the accused had changed significantly between the time when the offence was committed and the time when the accused was taken into that custody; or
	(c) it would not have been reasonable to have held an identification parade that included the accused.
(6) Sections 114(3), (4), (5) and (6) apply in determining, for the purposes of subsection (5)(c) of this section, whether it would have been reasonable to have held an identification parade.
(7) If picture identification evidence adduced by the prosecutor is admitted into evidence, the judge must, on the request of the accused—
	(a) if the picture of the accused was made after the accused was taken into that custody—inform the jury that the picture was made after the accused was taken into that custody; or
	(b) otherwise—warn the jury that they must not assume that the accused has a criminal record or has previously been charged with an offence.
Note
Division 4 of Part 4 of the Jury Directions Act 2015 also deals with warnings about identification evidence.
(8) This section does not render inadmissible picture identification evidence adduced by the prosecutor that contradicts or qualifies picture identification evidence adduced by the accused.
(9) This section applies in addition to section 114.
(10) In this section—
	(a) a reference to a picture includes a reference to a photograph; and
	(b) a reference to making a picture includes a reference to taking a photograph.


Evidence Act 2008 Dictionary
identification evidence means evidence that is—
(a) an assertion by a person to the effect that an accused was, or resembles (visually, aurally or otherwise) a person who was, present at or near a place where—
(i) the offence for which the accused is being prosecuted was committed; or
(ii) an act connected to that offence was done—
at or about the time at which the offence was committed or the act was done, being an assertion that is based wholly or partly on what the person making the assertion saw, heard or otherwise perceived at that place and time; or
(b) a report (whether oral or in writing) of such an assertion;


## Application
Evidence Act - Definition - Identification Evidence: In this case, the victim and witness are making an assertion that the accused was the person who committed the offence against them. 

s. 114(1): Were an identification parade held, it would have been considered visual identification evidence. However, the victim and witness have both made the identification via the photo board and therefore, this does not apply. 

s. 115(1): The evidence from the victim and witness is picture identification evidence, as it was made wholly/partly by the victim and witness examining pictures kept for police officers. 

s. 115(2): In this case, none of the photographs suggested anyone was in police custody.

s. 115(3)(a): In this case, the accused was in police custody at the time of the photo board being examined. However, the accused was not in custody for the same offence that he had been charged with. (3) would therefore not apply.

s. 115(4): Additionally to that argument (dependent upon interpretation of (3)), the accused had shaved his mohawk. Both the witness and victim gave a description of a man with a mohawk. I would submit that this is a significant change in appearance, especially when viewing photographs of persons faces on a photo board. Hair makes up a significant portion of the photograph. 

s. 115(5)(b): The accused appearance (as above) had significantly changed.

s. 115(5)(c): The informant had made earlier enquiries for approximately a month. It is therefore not reasonable for an identification parade (in addition to above) to have been held. 

## Conclusion
The evidence should be admitted. It was not reasonable to conduct an identification parade and the accused appearance had significantly changed.

# Question 7
You are prosecuting a contested hearing for the offence of unlawful assault.  

The allegation is that the accused was loitering in Federation Square in Melbourne CBD on a Friday night. The accused instigated a verbal argument with a male waiting for the tram, before slapping him to the face. Police were called and attended the scene where the accused was placed under arrest.

You call the informant and in her introductory evidence she outlines that she has been a general duties police member for 15 years and has worked at Melbourne East Police Station for the last 9 years. She details that her regular duties involve conducting foot patrols of licensed premises and surrounds on weekend nights to ensure public order.

In her evidence in chief the informant states that on her attendance she quickly observed that the accused was stumbling, slurring his words, and was drunk.

Defence object, claiming that the informant’s assertion that the accused was drunk is opinion evidence and is not admissible.

The magistrate asks for your response. What submission do you make about the informant’s evidence? Answer must be in IRAC format.

## Issue
Should the informants evidence be admitted? Is it opinion evidence? Does an exception exist?

## Rule
Evidence Act 2008 - s 76 The opinion rule
Evidence of an opinion is not admissible to prove the existence of a fact about the existence of which the opinion was expressed.

Evidence Act 2008 - s 79 Exception—opinions based on specialised knowledge
(1) If a person has specialised knowledge based on the person's training, study or experience, the opinion rule does not apply to evidence of an opinion of that person that is wholly or substantially based on that knowledge.
(2) To avoid doubt, and without limiting subsection (1)—
(a) a reference in that subsection to specialised knowledge includes a reference to specialised knowledge of child development and child behaviour (including specialised knowledge of the impact of sexual abuse on children and their development and behaviour during and following the abuse); and
(b) a reference in that subsection to an opinion of a person includes, if the person has specialised knowledge of the kind referred to in paragraph (a), a reference to an opinion relating to either or both of the following—
(i) the development and behaviour of children generally;
(ii) the development and behaviour of children who have been victims of sexual offences, or offences

## Application
s. 76: It is conceded that the informant's evidence goes towards proving the existence of a fact, namely that the accused was drunk (or the accused's state at the time he was arrested).

s. 79(1): Based upon the informants introductory evidence, the informants opinion is wholly based upon her extensive experience in dealing with drunk persons.

## Conclusion
The evidence is opinion evidence. There is an exception (s. 79(1)). It should be admitted.

# Question 9

You are preparing for a contested hearing (listed in 8 days’ time) for a single charge of driving in a dangerous manner.

You are contacted by the informant, who has today received a statement from defence. This statement purports to be a certificate of expert evidence and outlines the name, address, qualifications and states that a Dr Watson has specialised knowledge based on his training, study and experience. The substance of the evidence contained within the certificate is:

“I am an expert in automotive engineering. In my expert opinion the accused was not driving dangerously on this occasion.”

The informant sends you a copy and you see that defence have also served a notice that they intend to tender the certificate as opinion evidence as to the cause of the collision. A copy of the statement and the notice has been filed with the court. No further applications have been made in relation to the expert statement.

Can defence tender the certificate of expert evidence if the prosecution objects?

Answer in IRAC format.

## Issue
Can defence tender the certificate of expert evidence if the prosecution objects?

## Rule
Evidence Act 2008 - s 76 The opinion rule
Evidence of an opinion is not admissible to prove the existence of a fact about the existence of which the opinion was expressed.

Evidence Act 2008 - s 79 Exception—opinions based on specialised knowledge
(1) If a person has specialised knowledge based on the person's training, study or experience, the opinion rule does not apply to evidence of an opinion of that person that is wholly or substantially based on that knowledge.
(2) To avoid doubt, and without limiting subsection (1)—
(a) a reference in that subsection to specialised knowledge includes a reference to specialised knowledge of child development and child behaviour (including specialised knowledge of the impact of sexual abuse on children and their development and behaviour during and following the abuse); and
(b) a reference in that subsection to an opinion of a person includes, if the person has specialised knowledge of the kind referred to in paragraph (a), a reference to an opinion relating to either or both of the following—
(i) the development and behaviour of children generally;
(ii) the development and behaviour of children who have been victims of sexual offences, or offences

177 Certificates of expert evidence
(1) Evidence of a person's opinion may be adduced by tendering a certificate (expert certificate) signed by the person that—
(a) states the person's name and address; and
(b) states that the person has specialised knowledge based on his or her training, study or experience as specified in the certificate; and
(c) sets out an opinion that the person holds and that is expressed to be wholly or substantially based on that knowledge.
(2) Subsection (1) does not apply unless the party seeking to tender the expert certificate has served on each other party—
(a) a copy of the certificate; and
(b) a written notice stating that the party proposes to tender the certificate as evidence of the opinion.
(3) Service must be effected not later than—
(a) 21 days before the hearing; or
(b) if, on application by the party before or after service, the court substitutes a different period—the beginning of that period.
(4) Service for the purposes of subsection (2) may be proved by affidavit.
(5) A party on whom the documents referred to in subsection (2) are served may, by written notice served on the party proposing to tender the expert certificate, require the party to call the person who signed the certificate to give evidence.
(6) The expert certificate is not admissible as evidence if such a requirement is made.
(7) The court may make such order with respect to costs as it considers just against a party who has, without reasonable cause, required a party to call a person to give evidence under this section.

## Application
s. 76: The evidence being given is that of an automotive engineer's opinion about the accused's actions. He states that he did not commit the offence. 

s. 79: The opinion has been given under circumstances where Dr. Watson has specialised knowledge. This appears to be by way of a certificate. 

s. 117(1): In this case, Dr. Watsons evidence can be adduced via the certificate. It meets the requirements of (a), (b) and (c).

s. 117(2): Defence have served a notice along with the certificate that they intend to tender the certificate as evidence of Dr. Watsons opinion.

s. 117(3): However, Defence have not effected service within 21 days. The contested hearing is in 8 days. There have been no application made by Defence for service of a substituted period.

## Conclusion
No, defence cannot tender the certificate of expert evidence upon prosecution objected. Defence would need to succeed in a substituted period of service after the hearing.
