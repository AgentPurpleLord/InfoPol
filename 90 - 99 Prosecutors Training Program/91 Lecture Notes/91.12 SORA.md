# Failing to Comply with Reporting Obligations
- Most common
- Can only be non-authorised by an Inspector
- When accused placed onto the register, that are given a NORO (Notice of Reporting Obligations).
- Accused is given a NORO at each interview.

## How do we prove the accused failed to comply?
- Section 73 SORA - Certificate concerning evidence
	- A certificate produced by the sex offenders registry
	- Utilised to prove that they have no complied with their obligations.
	- E.g., the certificate from the registry would show the last known address provided at their compliance meeting. a brief would then contain an investigation as to how they did not show x-y-z.

- Application for this occurs **after sentencing**, as required by the SORA.
- In mention court, plea for a sex offence.
- Section 5 (2BC) Sentencing Act 1991
	- In sentencing an offender a court must not have regard to any consequences that may arise under the SORA 2004 or Working with Children Act 2005 for the imposition of sentence. 
	- Enter plea, accept summary, charge proven, sentenced. In the sentencing, Court **cannot have regard** to placing the offender onto the SOR.
	- E.g. if defence ask for a lesser sentence because of the hardship of the SOR, you must object and inform the court. 
- Section 6 of SORA sets out who a registerable offender is:
	- Any person who has been sentenced for a registerable offence.
	- Class 1 offences are found in Schedule 1 - serious sex offences against child - penetrative.
	- Class 2 offences Schedule 2 - sexual offence against a child (non penetrative)
	- Class 3 or 4 offence or any offence; an offence that results in the making of a sex offender registration order.

## Class 3 & 4 Offences
Class 3 offences are *discretionary*
- Person who is a serious sexual offender; penetrative offences against an adult. 

Class 4 offences are *discretionary*
- Person who is a serious sexual offender; Person who commits a sexual offences against an adult (non-penetrative offences against an adult).

A **serious sexual offender** is a person who has been sentenced for 2 or more offences listed in a Schedule in the Act s. 8(3) SORA.

# Juveniles - Discretionary Application
Found guilty of any offence; and
Prosecution must make an application; and
Court has discretion for all offences committed by juveniles; and
All reporting periods are half that of adult offenders with a maximum of 7 and a half years.

# Adults - Discretionary Registration
- Adult offender sentenced for any offence except for a Class 1 or Class 2 offence. 
- The prosecution must make application for the offender to be registered no more than 45 days after sentencing.
- With all discretionary registrations the court must be satisfied beyond reasonable doubt that the person poses a risk to the sexual safety of one or more persons of the community s 11(3).
- Bowden v The Queen and Sayer v The Queen are the relevant discretionary points of analysis:
	- Whether to make a registration order is a two-stage process.
	- First; is the court satisfied beyond a reasonable doubt that the person poses a risk to sexual safety as defined in the act.
		- The risk must be real.
		- Evaluation of the risk is when the offender is *released* not at the time of sentence.
	- Second; if satisfied BORG on risk, whether order should be made in all relevant circumstances.
		- Balancing the identified risk, having regard to the purpose of the Act, with restrictions imposed on offenders *right to enjoy freedom* and *autonomy of action*.
		- Consider the magnitude and nature of risk, degree of likelihood of the risk eventuating, gravity of harm, to be balanced against serious consequences for the offender.

## Risk to sexual safety of others
- The risk must be *real* and not *fanciful*.
	- As in it must be more than theoretical, remote or imagined.
- Example is R v Chan, aged 26 at time of offending had committed other sexual offences at age of 11-13. Psych evidence indicated slight chance of re-offending. This was not sufficient level of risk to justify making of a sex offender registration order.
- The risk must be *to the sexual safety of one or more persons or of the community*. Not necessary to identify a risk to a particular person or community group.
- The risk need not only be physical, this risk can also be psychological wellbeing. 

## Appropriate in all the circumstances
- The primary question is the *degree of risk*. 
	- Magnitude and nature of the risk; and
	- Gravity of the possible harm; and
	- Likelihood of the risk eventuating.
- Considerations must be balanced against the *onerous nature* of the reporting conditions that would be imposed. 
- An expert report may help the court assess the level of risk, but the absence of a report is not determinative.
- Faulty expert reports can lead to appealable error:
	- Sing v R, expert report re: reoffending referred to previous charge of similar nature. Previous charge was actually an earlier allegations that never went to trial. Court of Appeal held that use of the report, which was affected by discontinued charge, led judge to take into account irrelevant details. 

# Procedural

## Discretionary Offences
- VP Form 1142
- Verbal application made to the court.
- Requires submissions by the prosecutor to satisfy s 11(3) SORA
- May lead/call evidence to support application. 
	- Psych reports; or
	- Information from ROI; or
	- Witness statement, something said at the time, etc.

## Registerable Offences
- No paperwork attached to the brief.
- No requirement to make an application; but
	- Prosecutor should inform the court *after sentencing* that pursuant to SORA the accused is a RSO and then assist the court with regards to the applicable time period.

	- (a) if found guilty of a Class 3 offence, deemed to be a Class 1 offence
	- (b) if found guilty of a class 4 offence or any other offence, deemed to be a Class 2
