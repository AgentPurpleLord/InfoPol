# Question
You are assigned to prosecute a contested hearing where the accused has been charged with stalking. The accused and complainant had been in a 2-year relationship but the complainant ended the relationship one month before the charge date.
The charge relates to the accused allegedly following the victim around a shopping centre for several hours. The accused’s explanation in the ROI is that he was not following the complainant, he just happened to be shopping there himself. He did see the complainant but there was no reason for him to leave as there was no intervention order in place.
10 weeks prior to the hearing you served a notice on defence of your intention to adduce tendency evidence, which outlined the tendency to be alleged as “to act in a particular way namely, to monitor the whereabouts of the complainant”.
To support the tendency alleged the notice outlines the following evidence: 
The complainant will give evidence that when she was in a relationship, he was very jealous and would always want to know where she had been. This manifested in the following behaviours— 
On one occasion he had followed when she was out with friends. 
He also took her phone on a number of occasions to see who she had been texting and calling. 
If he didn’t know where she was, he would call her friends and work to try and locate her. 
Friends will give evidence that he called them looking for her on a number of occasions. 
At the beginning of the contested hearing the Magistrate, after looking at the tendency notice, asks you how you say the tendency evidence can be admitted into evidence. 
What is your response? (IRAC format preferred)

# Issue
Can this tendency evidence be admitted?

# Rule
97 The tendency rule
(1) Evidence of the character, reputation or conduct of a person, or a tendency that a person has or had, is not admissible to prove that a person has or had a tendency (whether because of the person's character or otherwise) to act in a particular way, or to have a particular state of mind unless—
(a) the party seeking to adduce the evidence gave reasonable notice in writing to each other party of the party's intention to adduce the evidence; and
(b) the court thinks that the evidence will, either by itself or having regard to other evidence adduced or to be adduced by the party seeking to adduce the evidence, have significant probative value.
(2) Subsection (1)(a) does not apply if—
(a) the evidence is adduced in accordance with any directions made by the court under section 100; or
(b) the evidence is adduced to explain or contradict tendency evidence adduced by another party.

Evidence Act Definitions
*probative value* of evidence means the extent to which the evidence could rationally affect the assessment of the probability of the existence of a fact in issue;

IMM v R (2016) 257 CLR 300, at 314: 
Cross on Evidence suggests [34] that a "significant" probative value is a probative value which is "important" or "of consequence". The significance of the probative value of the tendency evidence under s97(1)(b) must depend on the nature of the facts in issue to which the evidence is relevant and the significance or importance which that evidence may have in establishing those facts. So understood, the evidence must be influential in the context of fact-finding.

Hughes v The Queen [2017] HCA 20, at 41:
The assessment of whether evidence has significant probative value in relation to each count involves consideration of two interrelated but separate matters. 
The first matter is the extent to which the evidence supports the tendency. 
The second matter is the extent to which the tendency makes more likely the facts making up the charged offence. 
Where the question is not one of the identity of a known offender but is instead a question concerning whether the offence was committed, it is important to consider both matters. By seeing that there are two matters involved it is easier to appreciate the dangers in focusing on single labels such as "underlying unity", "pattern of conduct" or "modus operandi". In summary, there is likely to be a high degree of probative value where (i) the evidence, by itself or together with other evidence, strongly supports proof of a tendency, and (ii) the tendency strongly supports the proof of a fact that makes up the offence charged.

99 Requirements for notices
Notices given under section 97 or 98 are to be given in accordance with any regulations or rules of court made for the purposes of this section.

101 Further restrictions on tendency evidence and coincidence evidence adduced by prosecution
(1) This section only applies in a criminal proceeding and so applies in addition to sections 97 and 98.
(2) Tendency evidence about an accused, or coincidence evidence about an accused, that is adduced by the prosecution cannot be used against the accused unless the probative value of the evidence substantially outweighs any prejudicial effect it may have on the accused.
(3) This section does not apply to tendency evidence that the prosecution adduces to explain or contradict tendency evidence adduced by the accused.
(4) This section does not apply to coincidence evidence that the prosecution adduces to explain or contradict coincidence evidence adduced by the accused.

# Application
s. 97(1)(a) & s. 99: Notice has been served 10 weeks prior to this hearing, which is a reasonable time.

s. 97(1)(b): In this case, the evidence that the complainant and friends will give is of significant probative value (outlined below).

Evidence Act - Definition: The definition of probative value is outlined in the Rule section.

IMM v R (significant probative value): The Prosecution case is to prove that the accused stalked the complainant. Without this tendency evidence, which is of significant probative value, this charge cannot be proven beyond a reasonable doubt. The accused has argued he just happened to be in the same place and time as the complainant on this sole occasion. However, this tendency evidence would substantially influence the fact-finding by Your Honour, as it directly counters the accused's excuse. It demonstrates the accused's intent to track, monitor and follow the complainant in the past and currently.

Hughes v The Queen (2-step test):
First, the tendency evidence Prosecution seeks to adduce directly demonstrates a pattern of behaviour by the accused. The complainants testimony of the accused jealousy, his actions of following her, taking her mobile phone, contacting her friends and workplaces all in an effort to monitor her location. This repeated conduct supports the tendency alleged, which is that the accused intends to monitor the complainant's whereabouts. 

Second, the tendency evidence is crucial in supporting the charge of stalking. The charge alleges that the accused followed the complainant around the shopping centre. The accused denies this. The tendency evidence shows that the accused has a history of monitoring the complainant's movements. It makes it much more likely that on this occasion, he was in fact monitoring her again. By previously demonstrating this same behaviour, the tendency evidence makes the drawn inference here much more probable than were it not to be admitted. 

s. 101(1): This case is a criminal proceeding. We are seeking to adduce evidence by way of s. 97. s. 101 therefore applies.

s. 101(2): The probative value of the tendency evidence substantially outweighs any prejudicial effect on the accused. The evidence is crucial in proving the accused's pattern of monitoring and controlling behaviour. This directly supports the stalking charge. The court, as a professional adjudicator, can appropriately assess this evidence without being unduly influenced. Given its high probative value and relevance and Your Honours ability to navigate evidence, it should be admitted.

# Conclusion
The evidence should be admitted.
