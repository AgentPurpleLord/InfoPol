# Question
The accused is charged with assaulting his step-daughter. The complainant’s mother had observed bruising on her daughter’s legs and had taken her to see the family doctor for an examination at about 9am. The doctor observed injuries consistent with the allegation of assault. After seeing his last patient of the day, the doctor telephoned DHS to report his findings to a Child Protection worker. The doctor died the same evening in a car accident, without leaving any other record of his examination of the complainant.
Prior to the hearing, you have served defence with a notice to adduce hearsay evidence pursuant to s 67(1) of the Evidence Act 2008.
Defence object to the Child Protection worker giving evidence of what the doctor said on the basis that it is hearsay.
The magistrate asks you, “Is this evidence hearsay, if so, how is it admissible?”
Prepare a submission in response (IRAC format preferred).


# Issue
Is the Child Protection workers evidence hearsay?
Is the Child Protection workers evidence admissible?

# Rule
Evidence Act 2008 Dictionary - Representation
representation includes—
(a) an express or implied representation (whether oral or in writing); or
(b) a representation to be inferred from conduct; or
(c) a representation not intended by its maker to be communicated to or seen by another person; or
(d) a representation that for any reason is not communicated;

Evidence Act 2008 Dictionary - Previous representation
*previous representation* means a representation made otherwise than in the course of giving evidence in the proceeding in which evidence of the representation is sought to be adduced;

Evidence Act 2008 Dictionary - 4 Unavailability of persons
(1)     For the purposes of this Act, a person is taken not to be available to give evidence about a fact if—
	(a)     the person is dead; or
	(b)     the person is, for any reason other than the application of section 16 (Competence and compellability—judges and jurors), not competent to give the evidence; or
	(c)     the person is mentally or physically unable to give the evidence and it is not reasonably practicable to overcome that inability; or
	(d)     it would be unlawful for the person to give the evidence; or
	(e)     a provision of this Act prohibits the evidence being given; or
	(f)     all reasonable steps have been taken, by the party seeking to prove the person is not available, to find the person or secure his or her attendance, but without success; or
	(g)     all reasonable steps have been taken, by the party seeking to prove the person is not available, to compel the person to give the evidence, but without success.

Evidence Act 2008 - Section 59 The hearsay rule—exclusion of hearsay evidence
(1) Evidence of a previous representation made by a person is not admissible to prove the existence of a fact that it can reasonably be supposed that the person intended to assert by the representation.
(2) Such a fact is in this Part referred to as an asserted fact.
(2A) For the purposes of determining under subsection (1) whether it can reasonably be supposed that the person intended to assert a particular fact by the representation, the court may have regard to the circumstances in which the representation was made.

Evidence Act 2008 - Section 62 - Restriction to first-hand hearsay
(1) A reference in this Division (other than (2)) to a previous representation is a reference to a previous representation that was made by a person who had personal knowledge of an asserted fact.
(2) A person has personal knowledge of the asserted fact if his or her knowledge of the fact was, or might reasonably be supposed to have been, based on something that the person saw, heard or otherwise perceived, other than a previous representation made by another person about the fact.
(3) For the purposes of section 66A, a person has personal knowledge of the asserted fact if it is a fact about the person's health, feelings, sensations, intention, knowledge or state of mind at the time the representation was made.

# Application
s. 59: In this case, the Child Protection workers evidence is hearsay. It is evidence of a previous representation that was made by the now deceased Doctor. However, this evidence is admissible. 

s. 62: In this case, the evidence to be given by the Child Protection worker is first-hand hearsay. The Doctor told the Child Protection worker what he had seen during his medical examination of the victim.

Evidence Act Dictionary: In this case, the Doctor is considered to be unavailable pursuant to (a), the Doctor is deceased.

s. 65(2)(a): In this case, the Doctor phoned and spoke with the Child Protection worker as a result of of a duty that he was under.

s. 184(1): The Doctor was bound by duty to report his examination to Child Protection as a result of the physical injury to the child.

s. 182(1)(a): The Doctor is considered to be a 'mandatory reporter'. 

My submission on the facts are that the Doctor examined the victim and identified a physical injury on the child. That Doctor was then obligated by a duty to report those facts to Child Protection. The Doctor did that within the day and has then unfortunately passed before he could make a written record of his examination of the victim.

As the Doctor is deceased, he is an unavailable witness. The Child Protection worker can however provide this valuable evidence, as she has a first-hand hearsay account of the Doctor's findings. An exception exists for this hearsay evidence in that it was made by the Doctor who was under a duty to make that representation to the Child Protection worker as a 'mandatory reporter'.
# Conclusion
The evidence is hearsay, specifically, first-hand hearsay.
An exception exists and makes the Child Protection workers evidence admissible.
