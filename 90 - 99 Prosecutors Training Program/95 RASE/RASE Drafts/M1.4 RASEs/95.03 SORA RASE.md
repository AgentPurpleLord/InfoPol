# Application
Your Honour, Prosecutions is making an application pursuant to Section 11(1) of the Sex Offenders Registration Act (SORA), being a discretionary application, for a sex offender registration order to be made against the offender. This order would oblige him to provide information to police to prevent further offending and ensure police are aware of his whereabouts, in line with Section 1 of the SORA, the purpose of the Act.

I would first establish that the offender is a 'serious sexual offender' pursuant to Section 8(3) of the SORA, which provides that an offender who has been sentenced by a court for 2 or more offences listed in a Schedule within the SORA is a 'serious sexual offender'.

The offender has been found guilty of 3 counts of sexual assault in this matter. Schedule 4 of the SORA, Item 1 includes Section 40(1) of the Crimes Act 1958, being sexual assault. In following s. 8(3), these 3 offences would establish his status as a 'serious sexual offender'. 

In addition to this, the offender was previously found guilty of 4 counts of sexual assaults in 2022. In total, the offender has been found guilty of 7 counts of sexual assault. Without establishing this 'serious sexual offender' status, Prosecution would not be able to make a discretionary application under s. 11(1).

I now turn to the test that Your Honour must be satisfied of to make this order. Section 11(3) of the SORA provide: "The court may only make an order under this section if, after taking into account any matter that it considers appropriate, it is satisfied, beyond reasonable doubt, that the person poses a risk to the sexual safety of one or more persons or of the community."

Subsection (4) further provides that it is not necessary that the court be able to identify a risk to particular people, or a particular class of people. 

Relevant case law, being Bowden v The Queen, Sayer v The Queen and R v Chan, provide the following key points:
- The risk must be real and not fanciful.
- The risk must be assessed from the perspective of when the offender is released, not upon his sentencing.
- The risk and creation of the order must be balanced against the offenders human rights to autonomy and freedom.

In satisfying the court that the offender poses a real risk to the sexual safety of the one or more persons:
- All of the victims (both current and previous convictions) were not personally known to the victim. They were fleeting interactions and these assaults were therefore somewhat random in nature; and
- All of the offences occurred in public areas such as pubs, public transport, music festivals and outside of peoples homes; and
- The offender has never admitted that his behaviour was inappropriate to police. He has stated during interviews that he thought the victims were "interested" in him, or he has denied the allegations made by the victims entirely; and
- The offender was previously sentenced to an 18-month community corrections order, which failed to adequately rehabilitate him; and
- In the current offending, the police have struggled to locate the offender and in being unable to immediately arrest him, he has continued his offending against other victims. 

In making out this risk, I again return to Section 1 of the SORA. The order is sought to reduce the likelihood of re-offending by ensuring that the police know the offenders whereabouts and other personal information. 

It is based upon these submissions that Prosecution argue the risk is real, not fanciful, that if the offender were to be released back into the community without this order in place, he would pose a risk to the sexual safety of others in the community. 

I now turn to the matter of duration of the order. In determining the duration, Section 34(2) of the SORA provides that his previous convictions and current convictions should be taken into account, being 7 counts of sexual assault. 

Section 34(4)(b) provides that Class 4 offences are to be considered as Class 2 offences when calculating the duration of the order.

That leads me to Section 34(1)(c)(iii), in that the duration of the order for the offender should be the remainder of his or her life when the offender has been found guilty of 3 or more previous Class 2 offences. In this matter, the offender has been found guilty of 7 Class 2 offences (for the purpose of calculating duration).


# Conclusion
Prosecutions submits that the court make a sex offender registration order in relation to the offender for a duration of the remainder of his life.
