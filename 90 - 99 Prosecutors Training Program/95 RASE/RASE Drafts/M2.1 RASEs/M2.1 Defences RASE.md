
# Question
Learner Instructions 
You are prosecuting a contested hearing where the accused has been charged with driving whilst their authorisation was suspended on 3 separate occasions. In the course of the contested hearing the issue to be decided was whether the accused had a defence of mistake of fact available to them for each charge. It is conceded that at the time on each occasion the accused’s licence was suspended due to the accumulation of demerit points. The accused gives the following evidence for each charge: 
Charge 1 (date of offence 1/08/N-1) 
The accused was spoken to by police while driving and told that their licence was suspended. The accused stated they were unaware that they were suspended as they had not received any mail informing them that they were suspended. The accused accepted that they had received 10 speeding fines within 3 years leading up to their suspension and had paid those fines. They also accept that they received notices in relation to their demerit points but did not realise that they had exceeded their demerit point limit. The accused had not made any enquiries with VicRoads in relation to their licence. 
Charge 2 (date of offence 3/08/N-1) 
The accused was spoken to by police while driving and again stated that they were unaware that their licence was suspended as they had not received any mail informing them of this fact. 
Charge 3 (date of offence 1/09/N-1 
The accused was spoken to by police while driving in the car park of a shopping centre. They stated that they knew they were suspended on this occasion, but they were just moving the car for a friend. The accused stated they thought this was ok because they were not driving on the road. 
The Magistrate asks you for your submissions in relation to the defence raised by the accused. 
What is your response? Learners are required to prepare a written submission (IRAC format preferred).

# Issue
Does the accused have the 'mistake of fact' defence available to him in relation to these three charges?

**Proudman v Dayman (1941) 67 CLR 536**
(page 540 by DIXON, J) "It is one thing to deny that a necessary ingredient of the offence is positive knowledge of the fact that the driver holds no subsisting licence. It is another to say that an honest belief founded on reasonable grounds that he is licenced cannot exculpate a person who permits him to drive. As a general rule an honest and reasonable belief in a state of facts which, if they existed, would make the defendant's act innocent affords an excuse for doing what would otherwise be an offence."

(page 541 by DIXON, J) "The burden of establishing honest and reasonable mistake is in the first place upon the defendant and he must make it appear that he had reasonable grounds for believing in the existence of a state of facts, which, if true, would take his act outside the operation of the enactment and that on those grounds he did so believe. The burden possibly may not finally rest upon him of satisfying the tribunal in case of doubt. But in the present case, the applicant assigned reasons for her alleged belief which neither the magistrate nor the Full Court found convincing or sufficient. Indeed, it may be doubted if she thought at all upon the question whether the person she permitted to drive her car did or did not hold a subsisting licence."


**D P P v Foster [2000] VSC 32 (7 February 2000)**
16.The way the matter was argued before me by both counsel was that the issue was whether or not there were reasonable grounds for the holding of that belief by Foster, that he was entitled to drive as he believed that his licence was not suspended. It is apparent from the affidavit of the police officer in charge of the prosecution, Mr Mohammed, notwithstanding its absence from the transcript of the tape, that the magistrate relied upon Proudman v. Dayman [[1941] HCA 28](https://www.austlii.edu.au/cgi-bin/viewdoc/au/cases/cth/HCA/1941/28.html "View Case"); [(1941) 67 CLR 536](https://www.austlii.edu.au/cgi-bin/LawCite?cit=%281941%29%2067%20CLR%20536 "View LawCiteRecord") in forming the view that the charge must be dismissed, he in effect concluding that the defendant believed on reasonable grounds that he was entitled to drive, to put the matter in a more colloquial way.

17.The prosecution here once again sensibly accepted that it was virtually impossible to attack the magistrate's formation of the view that Foster believed that he was entitled to drive. Such a belief could be arrived at on the basis of having heard and observed Foster in the witness box. A person's beliefs are measurable only by external conduct, what they say and perhaps the probabilities or possibilities arising from the context. In any event it was accepted, and the magistrate found as a fact, that Foster did form that belief. That is not alone sufficient because the law does require the belief to be formed upon reasonable grounds. That does not however exclude elements personal and subjective to the individual.

Ignorance of the law does not excuse or relieve from the consequences of a crime: [_R v Taib; Ex parte DPP (Cth)_ [1999] 2 Qd R 649 at 663–664; (1998) 147 FLR 273 at 287–288; 158 ALR 744 at 757–758; 105 A Crim R 10 at at 663–664 (Qd R)](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U2&serNum=1998449327&pubNum=0005143&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&ppcid=4a1ba1f822e04f1e88549367adcb6ec7&contextData=\(sc.DocLink\)&comp=wlau#co_pp_663), 287–288 (FLR), 757–758 (ALR) and 24–25 (A Crim R) (CA).

[_CTM v The Queen_ (2008) 236 CLR 440; 185 A Crim R 188; 247 ALR 1](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U2&serNum=2016284522&pubNum=0003586&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&ppcid=4a1ba1f822e04f1e88549367adcb6ec7&contextData=\(sc.DocLink\)) Gleeson CJ, Gummow, Crennan and Kiefel JJ, quoting [_Ostrowski v Palmer_ (2004) 218 CLR 493; 206 ALR 422](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U2&serNum=2004585750&pubNum=0003586&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&ppcid=4a1ba1f822e04f1e88549367adcb6ec7&contextData=\(sc.DocLink\)), said (at 982; 193; 5 [7]):

> Mistakes of law are not a ground of exculpation: ignorance of the law is no excuse.

Wroblewski v Starling [1987] WAR 233
Held: (1) Once the basis for mistake was laid, the onus was on the prosecution to prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt. In the circumstances W's mistake in not realising he had had his licence suspended was not unreasonable.
(2) The mistake was one of fact and not law. The position would have been different if licence suspension had been an automatic effect of W's original offence.

McCaskie v Bagby (unreported, Supreme Court (WA), App 164 of 1976); Fletcher v Fowler (unreported, Supreme Court (WA), App 3 of 1985) _distinguished_.

# Application
s. 1 RSA & s. 17 RSA: In this matter, these two provisions provide the context of *why* drivers licences can be suspended or disqualified and the purpose of doing so.

s. 30AA RSA: In this matter, the accused has been charged 3 times with driving whilst suspended, pursuant to this section. The suspension was as a result of exceeding his demerit point limit. The accused was found by police in all three charges driving a motor vehicle on a highway.

s. 46G RSA: In this matter, this provision would give us the ability to introduce the required evidence, e.g. to provide evidence of service of notices and that his licence was suspended. (s. 84 of RSA would also enable us to provide certificate, etc.). These facts are also accepted by defence. 

Proudman v Dayman (1941) 67 CLR 536: In this matter, the accused has raised a defence of honest and reasonable mistake of fact to all three charges. I outline my submissions below as to why various elements of this defence are not adequately met.. 


In relation to Charge 1 (01/08/N-1)
s. 36 RSA: In this matter, the accused has a full drivers licence with a 12 demerit point limit (within a 3 year period), which is the standard. The accused made admissions to receiving 10 infringements within a 3 year period.

My submission is that whilst the accused may have a mistake of fact defence available to him, the element of that mistake being reasonable is not met. A reasonable person with a drivers licence is expected to know their demerit point limit. A reasonable person would also be expected to made enquiries with VicRoads as to the status of their licence after receiving 10 infringement notices.

In relation to Charge 2 (03/08/N-1)
My submission in relation to charge 2, is that the accused has again not met the elements of the reasonable and honest mistake of fact. When asked by Police if he was aware his licence was suspended, he told them that he was not. He made no mention to the Police of his intercept 2 days prior, in which he was informed of his suspension. The accused has therefore not met the element of the mistake being an honest mistake.

DPP V Foster [2000] VSC 32: In this matter, this case could have been relevant if the accused had raised why he didn't trust the police. However, there is no evidence before the Court as to the accused not trusting police and from his subjective view, he believed the police when they told him he was suspended. Additionally, the accused could have made reasonable enquiries with VicRoads over the two day period to verify what the police told him. 

In relation to Charge 3 (01/09/N-1)
RSA Definitions: A carpark would be considered a 'road' as per the definitions, as it is an area that is open to the public, used by the public and its main use is the driving of motor vehicles.

My submission in relation to charge 3, is that the accused does not have the defence of honest and reasonable mistake of fact available to him.
The accused made admissions that he was aware of his licence suspension. This was previously the fact in dispute.
The accused explained to police that he believed he was allowed to drive the vehicle in a carpark because it isn't a road. 
The issue in this charge is therefore a matter of law, particularly being the definition of a highway, road and road-related area.

# Conclusion
Charge 1: The mistake of fact was not reasonable.

Charge 2: The mistake of fact was not honest.

Charge 3: The mistake was one of law, not fact.
