## Charge 1 - Wilful Damage (s. 9(1)(c) Summary Offences Act 1966)
>(1) Any person who-
>(c) wilfully injures or damages any property (whether private or public) the injury done being under the value of $5000;
>shall be guilty of an offence. Penalty: 25 penalty units or imprisonment for six months.
1. The offence occurred at the *place* and *time* alleged (**CONCEDED**)
2. The offender *was the accused* (ID) (**CONCEDED**)
3. The accused *wilfully injured* or *damaged property*.
	1. **Circumstantially; Informant EIC; Tendency evidence.**
4. The value of the damage was *less than $5000*.
	1. **Statement of Martin TRENT**

*Wilfully injured/damaged* definition; Bergin v Brown [1990] VR 888, Orimston J said at [897]:
In the present case, however, the learned magistrate has used language consistent with his imposing a test based on negligence. In my opinion the word "wilfully" required the magistrate to be satisfied either that the accused intended to damage Mr Tullo's car or was reckless as to the consequence whether that car would be dismissed or not. What was essential to be proved was what was in the mind of the accused and not merely that of a reasonable person. An inference might have been drawn, but only if there was no other reasonable inference which could have been drawn to the contrary.

*Damage* defined at **R v Previsic (2008) 185 A Crim R 383 at 385**
It is not the law that there cannot be damage except if there is a cost of making good. The verb “damages” in [s 197(1)] has been given a broad meaning in like legislation. _Archbold Criminal Pleading, Evidence and Practice_, states that damage “is interpreted widely to include not only permanent or temporary physical harm, but also permanent or temporary impairment of value or usefulness”. Examples of damage given by Archbold include circumstances in which there would not necessarily have been cost of repair: applying water soluble paint to a pavement; and smearing mud on the wall of a police cell.

Damage involves a permanent or temporary alteration to the physical integrity of the property (Grajewski v DPP [2019] HCA 8, [13]).



## Charge 2 - Criminal Damage (s. 197(1) Crimes Act 1958)
> A person who intentionally and without lawful excuse destroys or damages any property belonging to another or to himself and another shall be guilty of an indictable offence and liable to level 5 imprisonment (10 years maximum).

1. The offence occurred at the *place* and *time* alleged (**CONCEDED**)
2. The offender *was the accused* (ID) (**CONCEDED**)
3. That the accused *destroyed* or *damaged property*.
	1. **Circumstantially; Informant EIC; Tendency evidence.**
4. That the accused did so *intentionally* and *without lawful excuse*.
	1. **Tendency; inference re: graffiti to photograph**
5. That the property destroyed or damaged *belonged to another person* or to the accused and another person.
	1. Statement of Martin TRENT (council)

*Damage* as above for **wilful damage; same case**; however in this charge the following intentional element must be proven.
*Intentionally* is defined at s. 197(4): 
> (4) For the purposes of subsections (1) and (2) a person who destroys or damages property shall be taken as doing so intentionally if, but only if:
>> (a) his purpose or one of his purposes is to destroy or damage property; or
>> (b) he knows or believes that his conduct is more likely than not to result in destruction of or damage to property.

*Property* is defined at s. 196(1) of CA 1958:
> property of a tangible nature, whether real or personal, including money and including wild creatures which have been tamed or are ordinarily kept in captivity and any other wild creatures or their carcasses if, but only if, they have been reduced into possession which has not been lost or abandoned or are in the course of being reduced into possession.

*Lawful excuse* is defined at s. 201(1) of the Crimes Act 1958:
> if the accused at the relevant time believed:
> (a) that the property belonged solely to himself or herself; or
> (b) that the accused had a right or interest in the property which authorised the accused to do what he or she did; or
> (c) that the persons whom he or she believed to be entitled to consent to the destruction or damage had consented; or
> (d) that if the persons whom the accused believed to be entitled to consent had known all the circumstances they would have consented.

**ONLY IF HUMAN RIGHTS RAISED RE FREEDOM OF EXPRESSION: Magee v Delaney (2012) 39 VR 50**
Para 1: The defendant was convicted in the Magistrates’ Court of one charge of damaging property contrary to s 197(1) of the Crimes Act 1958 (“the Act”) and one charge of possessing materials for the purpose of damaging property contrary to s 199(a)(i) of the Act. The defendant had painted over an advertisement in a municipal bus shelter and had been in possession of a bucket of paint and a paint brush for the purpose of painting over more advertisements. The defendant had sought to escape liability by contending that his acts engaged the right to freedom of expression in s 15(2) of the Charter of Human Rights and Responsibilities Act 2006 (“the Charter”), and that his exercise of that right in furtherance of his philosophical opposition to the global advertising industry constituted a “lawful excuse” for the purposes of ss 197(1) and 199(a)(i) of the Act. Section 15(3)(b) of the Charter subjected the right of freedom of expression to “lawful restrictions reasonably necessary … for the protection of … public order”. The defendant appealed against the conviction.
Para 3: In a case such as the present, whether an act was capable of imparting information or ideas was to be judged by its impact on reasonable members of the public who were exposed to it, without the benefit of any extraneous information about the purposes of the person who committed the act. If some reasonable members of the public would perceive that the act was seeking to convey one or more messages, then the act was capable of imparting information or ideas, even if they did not know, or were uncertain about, the precise message or messages sought to be conveyed. [63].
## Charge 3 - Mark Graffiti (s. 5 Graffiti Prevention Act 2007)
> A person must not mark graffiti on property if the graffiti is visible from a public place unless the person has first obtained the express consent of the owner, or an agent of the owner, of the property to do so. Penalty: Level 7 imprisonment.
1. The offence occurred at the *place* and *time* alleged **CONCEDED**
2. The offender *was the accused* (ID) **CONCEDED**
3. The accused *marked graffiti* on *property*.
	1. **Tendency evidence; accused is person that marked**
4. The graffiti was *visible from a public place*.
	1. **Visible from public path; INF EIC**
5. The marking was *without the express consent of the owner or owner's agent*.
	1. The accused has the evidential burden of raising the express consent of the owner.
	2. Statement Martin TRENT.

*Mark graffiti* is defined (s. 3 GPA 2007); to mean write, draw, mark, scratch or otherwise deface property by any means so that the defacement is not readily removable by wiping with a dry cloth.
*Public place* is defined (s. 3 GPA 2007); same meaning as Summary Offences Act 1966. would fit (a) any public highway road street bridge foot-way footpath court alley passage or thoroughfare notwithstanding that it may be formed on private property;
“Clause 5 of the Graffiti Prevention Bill interferes with a *person's right to freedom of expression* by making it an offence for a person to mark publicly visible graffiti on property without the consent of the owner of that property. However, the clause protects the property rights of the owner by requiring the property owner's express consent to the marking of the graffiti on their property. The clause is therefore a lawful restriction reasonably necessary to respect the rights and reputation of other persons, pursuant to s 15(3) of the Charter. 62”
