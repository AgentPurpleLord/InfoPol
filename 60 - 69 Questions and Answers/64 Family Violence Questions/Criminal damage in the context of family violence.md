# Overview
There can be confusion about *when to lay criminal damage charges* by police at family violence incidents. The primary issue is **ownership** of the property that is damaged.

# Issue
Can a Respondent be charged with *Criminal Damage*, pursuant to s. 197(1) of the Crimes Act 1958, when they have a *claim of right* to the property? — e.g. where the property is jointly owned, or where they believe they had a right to act as they did.

# Application
s. 197(1) requires the *property* be:
>"...property belonging to another or to himself and another..."

This **does not** preclude criminal liability if the property is co-owned. Damaging jointly owned property **can still be criminal damage** if the other elements of the offence are met.

s. 196(2)(a) defines property as belonging to someone who has **custody or control** over it. For example, even if the Respondent *legally owns* the property, the *custody or control* could lie with the partner — such as a jointly owned phone or vehicle in the partners possession, or damage to a home wall that the partner occupies.

s. 201(2)(a)(i-iv) of the Crimes Act 1958 provides the following four defences to Criminal Damage, which form *the key in family violence incidents*:
1. (i) the accused claims they believes the damaged property was *theirs alone*.
2. (ii) the accused believed they had a *right or interest* in the item that entitled them to damage or destroy it (e.g. phone under their name).
3. (iii) the accused believed the victim *would have agreed* to the damage if they knew the circumstances.
4. (iv) the accused says they damaged the property to *protect their own* or another's property from an immediate threat and their actions were reasonable in the circumstances.

s. 201(3) reinforces that the belief held by the Respondent **need not be reasonable**, only honest. But the belief **must exist at the time of the offence**.

As soon as the accused *raises a defence*, the Prosecution **must disprove it** beyond reasonable doubt. In combating each defence, we would consider seeking the following evidence:

## Defence 1: Belief that property belonged solely to the accused
s. 201(2)(a)(i)
- **Challenge the reasonableness of belief**: While an *honest belief* doesn't have to be *reasonable*, evidence that might contract the accused could undermine the credibility of that defence.
- **Evidence of co-ownership:** Do receipts, leases, bank statements or any other documents prove this co-ownership? Ensure victim statements clearly outline the background / ownership of the property.
- **Statements or admissions:** Does the accused always refer to it as "our" property (E.g. "our phone"), or does he place ownership on it ("I smashed her phone"). Are there old text messages or emails discussing it? Was it a gift? etc.
- **Shared use or location:** If the item was in a shared living space or used by both parties regularly, then it suggests co-ownership or shared control (e.g. one car for the family would be regularly used by all, even if only in one persons name).

## Defence 2: Belief in a right or interest that authorised the conduct
s. 201(2)(a)(ii)
- **Establish limits of that right:** A phone account in the accused's name on its own doesn't grant the right to physically destroy the phone. Consider how the phone, vehicle or property was purchased.
- **Intent matters:** Even if the accused has an interest or claim of right in the property; *what was their purpose* is the most important question. Was it damaged out of anger or control? Were they merely asserting the usual rights of an owner?
- **Context of violence:** If the property damage occurred during or following threats, arguments or intimidation, ensure it is clear in the statement. It weakens the legitimacy of the "right" asserted.
- **History of behaviour:** Prior arguments where the accused destroyed property as a control tactic could be admitted as tendency evidence to prove that the accused's belief was not honestly held if he raises it.  


# Scenario: The Smashed Phone
>[!INFO] Scenario
>During an argument, _Alex_ (husband) grabs _Jasmine’s_ phone and throws it against the wall, shattering it. The phone is **in her name**, but he claims he paid for it and has a right to control its use, saying she was “texting another man.”

- The accused has raised that the property is *jointly owned*. He pays for the phone, but the victim uses the phone regularly; it is her phone. 
- The accused has raised what could be considered a *lawful excuse* under s. 201(2)(a)(ii) — that he believed he had a right to damage the phone.
- Prosecution *must negate* this defence **beyond a reasonable doubt**.
- **How we might negate that defence:**
	- Statement from the victim which outlines what their arguments were about; what the accused said just prior to the phone being smashed, etc. 
	- Arguments from Prosecution in court that the accused had *other options available to him*; e.g. deleting the data, cancelling the phone plan, etc.
	- Look into the history of behaviour prior to the argument — has there been similar destruction as a control tactic in the past? Could this be introduced as tendency evidence — that the accused has a tendency to break the victims property when he doesn't like something about said property.


# Rule
In considering this question, the first place is to turn to the legislative provisions wording.
## Relevant Legislation
### Crimes Act 1958 Section 197 Destroying or damaging property
>(1) A person who **intentionally** and **without lawful excuse** **destroys** or **damages** any **property belonging to another** or to **himself and another** shall be guilty of an indictable offence and liable to level 5 imprisonment (10 years maximum).
>(4) For the purposes of subsections (1) and (2) a person who destroys or damages property shall be taken as doing so intentionally if, but only if—
>	(a) his **purpose** or **one of his purposes** is to **destroy** or **damage** property; or
>	(b) he **knows or believes** that his **conduct is more likely than not to result in destruction** of or **damage** to **property**.

Section 197(1) provides the provision that creates the offence. It requires Prosecution to satisfy the following elements:
1. That the offence occurred at the *place* and *time* alleged.
2. That the offender *was the accused*.
3. That the accused *destroyed* or *damaged property*.
4. That the accused did so *intentionally and without lawful excuse*.
5. That the property *destroyed* or *damaged* belonged to *another person* or *to the accused and another person*.

### Crimes Act 1958 Section 196 Definitions
>(1) In this subdivision— **property** means property of a tangible nature, whether real or personal, including money and including wild creatures which have been tamed or are ordinarily kept in captivity and any other wild creatures or their carcasses if, but only if, they have been reduced into possession which has not been lost or abandoned or are in the course of being reduced into possession
>**(2) For the purposes of this subdivision property shall be treated as belonging to any person—**
>	**(a) having the custody or control of it;**
>	**(b) having in it any proprietary right or interest (not being an equitable interest arising only from an agreement to transfer or grant an interest); or**
>	**(c) having a charge on it.**
>(3) For the purposes of this subdivision property which is subject to a trust shall be treated as belonging to the trustee or trustees and the person or persons who have a right to enforce the trust.
>(4) For the purposes of this subdivision property of a corporation sole shall be treated as belonging to the corporation notwithstanding a vacancy in the corporation.

Section 196(1) & (2) provide the definition of *property*. This is the only word that the legislative provisions defines within s. 197.

### Crimes Act 1958 Section 201 Lawful Excuse
> (1) This section applies to any offence under [section 197(1)](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U5&docFamilyGuid=Ib3e6b8dc035011e99495db3043f758b0&pubNum=1100190&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&docVersion=Law+in+Force&ppcid=4eb792ed54214e85abb499a9f62e7d06&contextData=\(sc.DocLink\)), [198(a)](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U5&docFamilyGuid=Ibba8ce7b035011e99495db3043f758b0&pubNum=1100190&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&docVersion=Law+in+Force&ppcid=4eb792ed54214e85abb499a9f62e7d06&contextData=\(sc.DocLink\)) or [199(a)(i)](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U5&docFamilyGuid=Ibba8ce70035011e99495db3043f758b0&pubNum=1100190&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&docVersion=Law+in+Force&ppcid=4eb792ed54214e85abb499a9f62e7d06&contextData=\(sc.DocLink\)) .
> (2) A person charged with an offence to which this section applies shall, whether or not he would be treated for the purposes of this subdivision as having a **lawful excuse** apart from this subsection, be treated for those purposes as having a lawful excuse—
> 	(a) if **at the time of the conduct** alleged to constitute the offence **he believed**—
> 		(i) that the property in question **belonged solely to himself**; 
> 		(ii) that **he held a right or interest in the property** in question **which authorized him to engage in the conduct**; or
> 		(iii) that the person or persons **whom he believed to be entitled to consent to the destruction or damage in question had so consented** or **would have so consented** if he or they had known the circumstances of the destruction or damage; or 
> 	(b) if he engaged in the conduct alleged to constitute the offence in order to **protect** property belonging to himself or another or a right or interest in property which was or which he believed to be vested in himself or another, and at the time of such conduct he believed— 
> 		(i) that the property, right or interest which he sought to **protect** was in **immediate need of protection**; and 
> 		(ii) that the **means of protection adopted** or proposed to be adopted were or would be **reasonable** having regard to all the circumstances.
> (3) **For the purposes of this section it is immaterial whether a belief is justified or not if it is honestly held.**
> (4) For the purposes of subsection (2) a right or interest in property includes any right or privilege in or over land, whether created by grant, licence or otherwise.
> (5) This section shall not be construed as taking away or otherwise affecting any other defence recognized by law as a defence to criminal charges.

Section 201 outlines only *some* of the lawful excuses available to the accused. In short summary they are:
1. At the time of the offence, the accused believed:
	1. The property belonged *only to himself*; or
	2. That they had a *right/interest in the property which authorised* them to damage/destroy the property; or
	3. They had *consent* to damage/destroy the property; or
	4. They *would have had consent from the owner(s) if they had known the circumstances* to damage/destroy the property.
2. At the time of the offence, the accused was protecting property that belonged to himself or another, and they believed:
	1. There was an *immediate need for protecting* the property; and
	2. The means that they protected the property was *reasonable in the circumstances*.

Most importantly, (3) provides that whether the accused's belief is *justified or not* is irrelevant. The accused must *only honestly hold the belief*.

### Howell v Dakin [1965] Tas SRpt 142
> The Police Offences Act 1935 by s. 37(1) provides that no person shall unlawfully destroy or injure any property. The agent of one of two co-owners of a house having been denied entry by the other co-owner, entered by breaking a window, an act which his principal had authorised him to do.
> Held that-
> (a) bona fide claim of right is no defence;
> (b) the authority of one co-owner is no defence
> (c) the Act prohibiting entry "with strong hand" is in Force in Tasmania
> (d) the principal had no right of forcible entry if he were ousted from the joint property
> (e) the other co-owner had in the circumstances acted reasonably in denying admission and
> (f) the applicant's act was unlawful
> *Page 144*: 
> The fact that the husband was a joint owner, likewise, affords no defence. Subsection (1) of s. 37 prohibits the unlawful destruction or injury of "any property". That this includes property jointly owned by a defendant and another is made plain by the form of subsection (2) of s. 37 which refers specifically to offences in respect of animals "the property of any other person". The argument of inconvenience cannot prevail against the plain words of the statute. 
> The appeal falls to be decided, then, on the short point: whether the appellant was acting unlawfully when he broke the window.
> The husband had a right to enter upon the property, and the law would protect this right by appropriate civil remedies. He could only bring an action for trespass, however, if he were ousted from the joint property (Wilkinson v. Haygarth(4); Murray v. Hall(5)).
> Exclusion is equivalent to ouster (Stedman v. Smith (6), and it may be that a temporary exclusion is a sufficient ouster to found trespass. But I do not think that there was sufficient evidence of an exclusion of the husband's agent by the wife. She could not reasonably be expected to open the door immediately at 2.30 a.m. to a man who was unknown to her, and whose claim to be her husband's agent depended at that stage on his own assertion. But if it were an ouster of the husband's agent it seems that he would not be entitled to exercise self-help because by so doing he would make an entry with "strong hand" in contravention of the statutes forbidding forcible entries.

A brief overview of Howell v Dakin [1965] Tas SR 142:
- The accused was convicted of unlawfully injuring a glass window on 14/06/1964.
- The accused was a private investigator employed to watch a wife to obtain evidence for divorce proceedings. The husband involved instructed the accused to keep watch on the house on Chapel Street, which was co-owned by Mr. and Mrs. Cutriss.
- The accused claimed he had authority to enter the house by force if necessary.
- At 2:30am, the accused knocked on the front door of the house. He told Mrs. Cutriss he was a private investigator and to open the door. Mrs. Cutriss refused. 
- The accused told Mrs. Cutriss if she did not open the door, he would smash the window. Mrs. Cutriss didn't open the door and he smashed the window.

This case provides insight into the role of *joint ownership* of property, namely, the window. Mrs. Cutriss needed to consent to the damage as well in order for it to not be a crime.

## Scenario 1: The Smashed Phone
>[!INFO] Scenario
>During an argument, _Alex_ (husband) grabs _Jasmine’s_ phone and throws it against the wall, shattering it. The phone is **in her name**, but he claims he paid for it and has a right to control its use, saying she was “texting another man.”

**That the offence occurred at the *place* and *time* alleged.**
Police have attended that place and obtained a statement from Jasmine about the time. These issues are not disputed by the accused.

**That the offender *was the accused*.**
Identity is not in issue, they are known to each other.

**That the accused *destroyed* or *damaged property*.**
The phone was shattered. Jasmine's statement provides evidence of this. Police also took photographs, which corroborates that it was damaged.
The phone is considered 'property' under s. 196 of the Crimes Act 1958.

**That the accused did so *intentionally and without lawful excuse*.**
The accused meets the definition of intentionally in his actions under s. 197(4) of the Crimes Act 1958. One of his purposes was to smash the phone. He may have had another purpose, e.g. to vent his anger, but what other purpose can one have when throwing a phone at a wall?

The accused has raised an argument of a *lawful excuse* because he believes that his payment for the phone makes it his property. First, lets work through s. 201:
- The property *did not belong only to him*; and
- The accused had *some right/interest* in the phone, but not one that would authorise him to damage it; and
- The accused *did not have consent* to damage the phone; and
- The victim *would not* have consented in those circumstances.

The argument would hinge upon whether or not this belief was *honestly held*. 


There are three offences created by Section 197:
- s. 197(1) being the standard 'Criminal Damage' charge; and
- s. 197(2) typically covers fires or similar, it's damage that endangers the life of someone; and
- s. 197(3) covers insurance fraud type of offences, being damage with a dishonest intent/view to gain.

In a family violence context, we are dealing with s. 197(1). This requires the following elements to be proven:


For the purposes of applying this scenario, we can assume that common things damaged as a result of a family violence incident might be (all of these meet the definition of *property* found at s. 196(1)):
- Furniture, appliances or household items (plates, cups, etc); or
- Property itself such as doors, walls, etc; or
- Motor vehicles.

## The accused destroyed or damaged property
Whilst it is focusing on New South Wales legislation, the High Court decision of **Grajewski v Director of Public Prosecutions (NSW) [2019] HCA 8** provides a clear outline of damage (as above). Using the above items, we could say they could be damaged by:
- A piece of furniture being broken in half, e.g. a chair's legs being ripped off or a vase being smashed on the floor and shattering; or
- A door or wall having holes punched into it; or
- A motor vehicle being scratched (keyed), panels punched, windows smashed, etc.

## The accused did so intentionally
s. 197(4) of the Crimes Act 1958 (as above) clearly explains what *intentionally* means for this offence. 

In applying it to a basic example where an Affected Family Member and Respondent have an argument at the dinner table. The Respondent loses his temper and throws his dinner plate against a wall smashing it.

We would argue that this was intentionally, even if he was 'blinded by rage' because:
- At least *one of his purposes* was to destroy or damage the plate. They may also have had another purpose, to vent his anger, to intimidate the affected family member, etc, but as long as *one* purpose is to destroy or damage, it's considered *intentional*; or
- They ought to have known or believe that throwing the plate against a wall would result in it being destroyed or damaged.

## The accused did not have a lawful excuse
This element of the offence ties directly into the last element; who was the owner of the property? The lawful excuses that exist that an accused can raise to a charge of Criminal Damage are found at s. 201 of the Crimes Act 1958:

- The accused believed the property belonged solely to himself; or
- The accused believed that he held a right or interest which would have authorised him to engage in the conduct; or
- The accused believed they had consent to damage/destroy the property in question or that he would have had consent from the owner if the owner knew the circumstances of the damage/destruction; or
- The accused was protecting property that he had a right/interest in; and the means of protection the accused utilised were reasonable having regard to the circumstances.

For our purposes, I will focus on the ownership elements of the lawful excuse below.

## A lawful excuse: ownership or right of ownership
In order for the accused to have this defence available to them:
1. The accused must believe at the *time of the offence* that the property *belonged solely to him*; or

>[!IMPORTANT] Scenario
>The Respondent lives with the AFM at their house. The Respondent is the registered owner of a Honda Civic, the family vehicle. They have been married for several years and have children. The Respondent gets into an argument with the AFM about her usage of the car because he thinks she isn't a fuel economic driver. The Respondent punches the exterior door panel of the car dinting it in a fit of rage.
>
>During the Respondent's record of interview, the says he believed that the AFM consented to this damage, because he said, "You don't care at all about the car!" and she replied, "No, I don't give a fuck about the car."

This is essentially an argument that the accused had a lawful excuse, being a *belief in consent* pursuant to s. 201(2)(a)(iii) that he was entitled to cause the damage. In the case of R v Denton this was tested. The defence was only allowed when a person was *clearly instructed to cause the damage*, as an employee had been instructed to set fire to a factory (and the employee was not convicted of criminal damage due to having a legitimately held belief he had consent).

Consent of damage in this circumstance must be *clear*. Prosecution must be able to prove that the belief held by the accused that consent was provided was not *honestly held*.
