## Issue 1: Tickbook
- The tickbook was located in the top bed-side drawer of the accused's bedroom. 
- Within this top drawer, there is a small plastic container. The tickbook is inside this. On top of the tickbook is the old police ID.
	- The accused states in his interview he received this ID two days ago, meaning that this area of the drawer was recently disturbed by him placing it there.
	- There are also other items supporting the circumstances of trafficking in this same drawer: scales, deal bags, a box with a phone and multiple sim card, etc.
- The tickbook contains several pages of sales.
	- The sales are listed as $XXX 2 NAME, often with a quantity listed. e.g. 112g, 3pts (still owes), 100ml, etc.
	- The only pages with dates are pages for passwords, presumably, these dates refer to when that password was set or similar? There are no alleged drug transactions on these pages.
- From Ross on Crime regarding the equipment or implement of crime:
	- "The admissibility generally turns on whether the equipment is capable of being used for the crime in question. The evidence is not admissible if it does no more than show that the accused has a propensity to commit crimes of that sort."
	- _In_ [_R v Edwards_ (1993) 67 A Crim R 539 (Vic CCA)](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U2&serNum=1993408754&pubNum=0005341&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&ppcid=1df22d8312e848a398beabd9587988b8&contextData=\(sc.Keycite\)) on charges of trafficking cannabis, evidence was held to be properly admitted of the possession of scales and plastic bags.
- Tick Books have clearly been allowed previously into evidence:
	- From Uniform Evidence Law Section 101:
	- "Thus, an accused may be found in possession of items, or engaged in certain activity, which supports an inference that the person was engaged in criminality. This use of the evidence does not entail tendency reasoning because the evidence is not relied upon to establish a tendency from which an inference of conforming conduct is drawn. Rather, possession of the items or participation in the activity permits a direct inference to criminality. Nor does this use of the evidence necessarily entail coincidence reasoning. A direct inference to criminality does not rely on improbability of coincidence. In [_R v Falzon_ (2018) 264 CLR 361; 92 ALJR 701; [2018] HCA 29](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U2&serNum=2045218479&pubNum=0003586&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&ppcid=a5f4cfb23b1945cabc455040593778a8&contextData=\(sc.Search\)), the High Court (Kiefel CJ, Bell, Keane, Nettle and Gordon JJ) observed at [1] that, where a person is found in possession of drugs and charged with possession with intent to sell the drugs, evidence that the person was also found in possession of “indicia” of drug trafficking (such as scales, re-sealable plastic bags, firearms, a multiplicity of mobile telephones or significant quantities of cash) will not be caught by the tendency rule. Thus, the court stated at [42] that evidence that a large amount of cash was found in another of the respondent's homes was not tendency evidence because the purpose of its admission was not to establish that the respondent was the sort of person who was disposed to commit acts of trafficking but rather to establish that the respondent was in fact carrying on a business of trafficking and, therefore, that the respondent's intention was to sell the drugs."
- **Prosecution Position**: The tick book can be relied upon as evidence which is admissible and an inference can be drawn in the circumstances that it supports the charges of trafficking in meth and ghb.

## Issue 2: Weight of Drugs
- R v Coviello (1995) 81 A Crim R 293 speaks to this somewhat:
	- "In determining the weight of the drug it is appropriate to make the measurement in the light of the conditions existing at the time that the offence is seen to be committed. Thus, if a crop is “green” it is its weight in such condition which is to be measured. The crop is not to be taken as that which it would be when “dried” even though the drug only becomes usable when in that condition: cf Tsesmetzsis (unreported, Court of Criminal Appeal, 28 April 1980)."
- Admittedly, the above refers to Cannabis crop being wet at the time and this is perhaps not a fair comparison. 
- In relation to the ziplock bags, I do not believe the weight can be contested. A ziplock bag would not appear on scales.
- Regarding small glass jars:
	- I've had a look online and a 150ml jar would weight approximately 80 grams (a pack of 24 jars was a net weight of 3kg on average).
	- Either way, the basis of the charges would be made out, e.g. traffickable quantiy of GHB and Meth, given the amount that is there. GHB MUST be weighed in a container as well, as it is a liquid. 
- Charmy has previously raised the issue that the quantities here might be exceeding the commercial quantity, once they get weighed, we could uplift this matter.
