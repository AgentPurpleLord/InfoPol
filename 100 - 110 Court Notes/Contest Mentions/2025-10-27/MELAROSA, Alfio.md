	- *Section 12, 'Use of Speed Detectors'*
		- All speed detectors are to be used in accordance with their operational requirements.
		- Prescribed speed detectors are listed in r.41 of the Regulations.
		- A mobile radar can only be used in mobile mode on a road that is not in *a built-up area*. A mobile radar is not to be used on divided roads in Opposite Direction Mode
	- VPM does not define *built-up area*.
	- *Road Rules 2017* does define *built-up area*
> "built-up area", in relation to a length of road, means an area in which either of the following is present for a distance of at least 500 metres or, if the length of road is shorter than 500 metres, for the whole road—
>	(a) buildings, not over 100 metres apart, on land next to the road;
	  (b) street lights not over 100 metres apart;

Kingman v Seaeger 1938 1 KB 397 is a case where the accused was driving a lorry in excess of the speed-limit, but it was dismissed when the lower court found that there was not 'real' danger to a 'real' member of the public. The subsequent appeal overturned that interpretation. Humphreys J at [400] said:

>If, on such a statement of facts as we have here, justices are to be at liberty to say that the offence of dangerous driving is not proved, then no person can be convicted of that offence unless there has actually been an accident or the probability of one...

>...It would be quite absurd to say that there are not some speeds which in themselves must be dangerous to the public...

>... And **when the speed is found to be at times double the lawful speed** on a road such as one is dealing with in the present case, it is obvious there can be no justification for the justices declining to find that a speed has been proved which was in itself a dangerous speed.


## Mazza v Haime [2000] WASCA 42
Mazza v Haime [2000] WASCA 42; appeal after found guilty of driving motorcycle at a speed in excess of 112km/h; speed was dangerous to his pillon (backseat) passenger; speed limit was 60km/h; built-up area; single lane traffic either direction; houses either side; time of day was rush hour for work; 
HENNAN J stated at [10]:
>As the wording of s 60(1) shows, the speed at which a motor vehicle is driven in itself can constitute a danger to the public. Even on an open road with a high speed limit, the likelihood of a sudden emergency always is present. Kangaroos, cattle or other animals can suddenly appear on the road only a short distance ahead. Branches of trees or the trees themselves might fall on or in front of the vehicle. In a built up area the likelihood of a sudden emergency is much greater. Members of the public generally do not expect speed limits to be ignored. When a motor vehicle exceeds the limit they are likely to take precipitate or even foolish action. A child might suddenly run on to the road. An adult might misjudge the speed of the approaching vehicle and step into its path. A car might suddenly appear from a driveway or a side road.

And at [11]:
>To drive as fast as the appellant did greatly increases the risk of accident to the occupants of a vehicle - especially if it is a motorcycle - or to other persons who happen to be in the vicinity. Having regard to all the circumstances of the case I find myself unable to conclude that her Worship was wrong in deciding that the driving was dangerous.
