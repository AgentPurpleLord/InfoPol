# The issue of suspicion
Murray, Hale and Olsen v The Queen [2017] VSCA 236 talks to the practical aspects of engaging s 82 DPCSA. In that case:
>The applicants, intercepted by police while driving a BMW late at night, were searched without a warrant under s 82 of the _Drugs, Poisons and Controlled Substances Act 1981 (Vic)_. Police located drugs and cash. The trial judge ruled the search was unlawful due to lack of reasonable suspicion but admitted the evidence under s 138 of the _Evidence Act 2008 (Vic)_.

[66]
>As we have mentioned, s 82 of the Act contemplates circumstances in which police have reason to suspect the existence of a drug of dependence on a person or in a vehicle, but where it would not be practical for the police to be able to apply for a warrant to search the person or vehicle. Not uncommonly, police would be required to undertake a search in circumstances such as those that took place in the present case, namely, where the police have occasion to intervene, or intercept a vehicle, and in circumstances in which there are grounds for suspecting that the person or vehicle, or both, are holding illegal drugs of dependence. Thus, the context of the particular search must be taken into account, and given appropriate weight, when the question arises whether the police had reasonable grounds for suspecting that there is a drug of dependence in a vehicle or in the possession of a person in a public place.

[76]
>As we have noted, Sergeant Jones, and Constable Smith, each formulated their suspicion, that there were drugs on or about the applicants or the vehicle, in the course of the dynamic circumstances which unravelled after the BMW vehicle, containing the three applicants, cut across the path of the police vehicle in Derby Street. It could be fairly accepted that, as the vehicle accelerated hard away from the police vehicle at that stage, the police were justified in entertaining suspicions about the vehicle, or the occupants of it. Those suspicions would have been strengthened by the conduct of the vehicle in turning left into Kent Street, while still travelling at speed, and then left again into Sunshine Street, in order to evade apprehension or interception by the police. Further, the conduct of the vehicle, in then stopping in Sunshine Street, could reasonably give rise to an apprehension that the occupants were seeking to dispose of, or secrete, some item or thing which, if discovered by the police, might incriminate them. That suspicion would have been enhanced by the conduct of Olsen in alighting from the vehicle, and commencing to walk away from it. It would have been further fortified by the false explanation given by Olsen for the fast and erratic manner in which the vehicle had been driven, and for his being out of the vehicle at the time the police intercepted it. In addition, the police were well justified in being sceptical about the explanation by the occupants of the vehicle, that they were hurrying to attend a dinner in Brunswick at 11:00 pm on a Monday night.

The issue that comes from this case and which would apply in this matter, is that of a deliberate breach or ignorance of s 82 provisions. 

The principles of SORG in relation to s 82 drug searches is discussed in the case of Murray, Hale and Olsen v The Queen [2017] VSCA 236:

The applicants, intercepted by police while driving a BMW late at night, were searched without a warrant under s 82 of the _Drugs, Poisons and Controlled Substances Act 1981 (Vic)_. Police located drugs and cash. The trial judge ruled the search was unlawful due to lack of reasonable suspicion but admitted the evidence under s 138 of the _Evidence Act 2008 (Vic)_.

Essentially, the suspicion of the Sergeant was formed on the following grounds:
- The attitude of the owner of the vehicle (Olsen) was 'almost identical' to a person who he knows who is a convicted drug dealer.
- When the Sergeant arrived, he saw Olsen had already left the vehicle and believed he had done so to dispose of something.
- The Sergeant believed that all three persons in the vehicle displayed nervous behaviour, such as squiring and fidgeting.
- The Sergeant had observed the vehicle turn sharply down a side-street and a believed speed of 60.

In this specific matters, I can identify the following as the reasons for the SORG being formed by the attending police:
1. The time of day, being in the very early hours of the morning.
2. The amount of people in the vehicle, with no apparent association (e.g. its not someone driving their kids home from a party).
3. They appeared nervous to police.

In applying these principles, it might be the case that the search was conducted wrongfully, it may not. 

# Public Interest Factors
The offence is minor, being the possession of 10-20ml of GHB. The offender was co-operative with the investigation, but did not make admissions to the matter. 

There is no victim for this matter.

There is a strong need for the community protection from drugs, as well as the need for a general deterrence to drive a motor vehicle around with readily available drugs. 

However, I believe prosecuting this matter would be seen as counter-productive and would bring Victoria Police members into disrepute. There would likely be a lengthy trial on the issue of suspicion on reasonable grounds. Whilst the police officers background in dealing with drug users could be used pursuant to the above authority, it might still be ruled as inadmissible under s 138 of the Evidence Act. 

Given the minor nature of the offence balanced against the possible disrepute, I have taken the position that this matter be withdrawn.
