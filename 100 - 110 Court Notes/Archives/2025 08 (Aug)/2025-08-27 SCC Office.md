| 1   | Handle Stolen Goods | W/D        |

Justin Carthy was arrested on 13 June 2024. His phone was searched. On the phone was a video recording of a Jayco Caravan suspected of being stolen in a burglary at 3:49am that morning.

On the same day at 1:00pm, the accused was located inside the caravan asleep on Dukelows Road, Mount Cottrell. The caravan was 9.0km away from the location it was stolen from, inside Cobbledick’s Ford Streamside Reserve.

The accused refused to be interviewed in relation to the matter.

Defence argue that prosecution must prove Mr. BURROWS had knowledge that the caravan was stolen to satisfy the offence. Defence submits that there is no evidence to support this.

**Handle Stolen Goods**

1. The offence occurred at the time and place alleged.
2. The offender was the accused.
3. That the accused:

4. received goods or brought them into Victoria; or
5. undertook or assisted in bringing goods into Victoria; or
6. undertook or assisted in the retention, removal, disposal or realisation by or for the benefit of another person of goods; or
7. arranged to do any of the things in paragraphs (a)–(c) above.

8. That the accused did so dishonestly.
9. That the accused did so knowing or believing the goods to be stolen goods.

**Knowing or believing the goods to be stolen**

Atwal v Massey [1971] 3 All ER 881 (a UK case) discusses this. Lord Widgery CJ said:

“If when the justices say that the appellant ought to have known that the kettle was stolen they mean that any reasonable man would have realised that it was stolen, then that is not the right test. It is not sufficient to establish an offence under s 22 that the goods were received in circumstances which would have put a reasonable man on his inquiry. The question is a subjective one: was the appellant aware of the theft or did he believe the goods to be stolen or did he, suspecting the goods to be stolen deliberately shut his eyes to the consequences …”

The specific phrase of “deliberately shut his eyes to the consequences …” is a contentious one. It’s been discussed in other cases:

 [_R v Grainge_ [1974] 1 WLR 619; [1974] 1 All ER 928](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?findType=Y&serNum=1974026741&pubNum=0004891&originatingDoc=Icaaefeab0b4011ea83b0ac48dfa2554a&refType=IC&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&ppcid=ce34c474cdfb422b97f8bdd006e80f9e&contextData=\(sc.Keycite\)):

“Lord Widgery CJ was not seeking to introduce another definition of the offence, but was examining the possible approaches made by the justices on the question which they had to decide and he was emphasizing at the same time that the mental element was subjective.”

“[T]he recorder ought to have made plain that it was at the moment of receipt and not at any time during the handling thereafter that the guilty knowledge had to be proved.”

 [_R v Griffiths_ (1974) 60 Cr App R 14](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?findType=Y&serNum=1974026742&pubNum=0004705&originatingDoc=Icaaefeab0b4011ea83b0ac48dfa2554a&refType=IC&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&ppcid=ce34c474cdfb422b97f8bdd006e80f9e&contextData=\(sc.Keycite\)&comp=wlau)

“There is a danger in the adoption of the passage cited from the judgment of _Atwal v Massey_ (supra) as the direction to a jury unless great care is taken to avoid confusion between the mental element of knowledge or belief and the approach by which the jury may arrive at a conclusion as to knowledge or belief and the approach by which the jury may arrive at a conclusion as to knowledge or belief. To direct the jury that the offence is committed if the defendant, suspecting that the goods were stolen, deliberately shut his eyes to the circumstances as an alternative to knowing or believing the goods were stolen is a misdirection. To direct the jury that, in common sense and in law, they may find that the defendant knew or believed the goods to be stolen because he deliberately closed his eyes to the circumstances is a perfectly proper direction.”

**My position on this**

I can understand your argument, e.g. he was found sleeping in the caravan, how can we therefore prove that he knew or believed the goods were stolen _at the time he came across them_. But this is where my position diverges. It is a circumstantial case, but it’s a strong circumstantial case. The accused refused to provide a story, as is absolutely his right. If we adduce the evidence from the Sergeant who arrested him, he is found inside an _unlocked_ caravan in a reserve.

When it comes to inferences, I like to rely upon the ‘Hodge’ Direction:

_“To find the accused guilty, his or her guilt must not only be a reasonable inference, it must be the only reasonable inference which can be drawn from the circumstances established by the evidence; and If the jury considers that there is any reasonable explanation of those circumstances which is consistent with the innocence of the accused, they must find him or her not guilty.”_
