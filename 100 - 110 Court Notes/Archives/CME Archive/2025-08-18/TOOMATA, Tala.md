- Self defence

In [_Zecevic v DPP (Vic)_ (1987) 162 CLR 645; 25 A Crim R 163; 71 ALR 641](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U2&serNum=1987180337&pubNum=0003586&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&ppcid=9227976116df412685b2921d208168eb&contextData=\(sc.Category\)) Wilson, Dawson and Toohey JJ said (at 664; 654; 175):

> The whole of the surrounding circumstances are to be taken into account and where an accused person has created the situation in which force might lawfully be applied to apprehend him or cause him to desist–where, eg, he is engaged in criminal behaviour of a violent kind–then the only reasonable view of his resistance to that force will be that he is acting, not in self-defence, but as an aggressor in pursuit of his original design. A person may not create a continuing situation of emergency and provoke a lawful attack upon himself and yet claim upon reasonable grounds the right to defend himself against that attack.

These principles are still relevant to the application of the statutory self-defence provisions in Victoria. To this end, the it was noted in [_Edmunds v The King_ [2025] VSCA 31](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U2&serNum=2083346540&pubNum=0006102&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&ppcid=9227976116df412685b2921d208168eb&contextData=\(sc.Category\)) (at [211] to [214] that:

> The statutory provisions are consistent with the underlying requirement that self-defence must involve the accused responding in defence of himself to the conduct of the victim:
> 
> Under s 322K(2)(a), the accused must believe that the conduct is necessary in self-defence. As the authorities cited above show, this involves the accused believing that the response was necessary in defence of himself.
> 
> Under s 322K(2)(b), the conduct must be “a reasonable response in the circumstances as the person perceives them”.[50] Self-evidently, this explicitly demands that the conduct of the accused is responsive to the conduct of the victim.
> 
> Section 322L provides that self-defence does not apply if the accused “is responding to lawful conduct”[51] of the victim. While s 322L is directed at the question of the lawfulness of the conduct of the victim, it is predicated upon the anterior or threshold fact that the accused responded to that conduct.
> 
> Section 322I(2) provides that if the accused satisfies the relevant evidential onus, “the prosecution has the legal onus of proving beyond reasonable doubt that the accused did not carry out the conduct in self-defence”.[52] Again, this reinforces that the accused's conduct must have been carried out for a defensive purpose in response to the threat posed by the victim.
> 
> This brings us to our fourth (albeit related) observation: self-defence cannot be used as a pretext to carry out a criminal purpose.[53]
> 
> If a person kills in pursuit of an original criminal design to commit murder, it could hardly be said the person acted in self-defence. In these circumstances, it is difficult to see how they could have responded to the conduct of the victim and formed the belief that it was necessary to engage in the fatal act to defend themselves from the risk of death or really serious injury.
> 
> Certainly, the justificatory or exculpatory effect of self-defence[54] would be entirely subverted if it could be invoked by a person who planned and fully intended to harm the victim in a particular way and succeeded in doing precisely that, and so acted irrespective of any action taken by the victim.
