Regarding recent possession evidence, see:
In Trainer v The King (1906) 4 CLR 126 at 132, Griffith CJ said:
>It is a well known rule that recent possession of stolen property is evidence either that the person in possession of it stole the property or received it knowing it to have been stolen, according to the circumstances of the case. Prima facie the presumption is that he stole it himself but if the circumstances are such as to show it to be impossible that he stole it, it may be inferred that he received it knowing that someone else had stolen it. This is only an illustration of the rule as to circumstantial evidence.

And regarding 'recency' given it was one month, there are the following cases and timelines where it was considered to be 'recent':
R v Saleam (1989) 16 NSWLR 14; 39 A Crim R 406 at 113 (NSW CCA);
R v Mahoney (2000) 114 A Crim R 130 at 138 [31] (NSW CCA);
A horse, within a year: R v Croton and Norris (1911) 5 QJPR 219 (Dickson ADCJ);
A dress, nine months after the theft: R v McCaffrey [1911] VLR 92 (FC);
Jewellery, six weeks after the theft: McCarthy v The Queen [1985] WAR 84; (1984) 13 A Crim R 13 (CCA);
Jewellery, 11 weeks after the theft: R v Beljajev [1984] VR 657 (CCA).

## Withdrawal Narrative
Charge 1 - Handle Stolen Goods - W/D

Matter relates to an incident where a citizen has called 000 for an outstanding stolen registration plate on a vehicle parked out the front of an address in Hoppers Crossing.

Police attended the address and checked both the plates and VIN of this vehicle. The VIN returned as the owner being the accused and that it was unregistered for almost a year prior to that date. 

Police knocked on the door of the address and spoken to a resident (unnamed on the brief) who has nominated the accused as the owner of the vehicle. The accused then came to the front door and claimed ownership of the vehicle. He was arrested and taken to the Werribee Police Station for interview.

During interview, the accused stated he was never in possession of the stolen registration plates. He said that the vehicle was non-operational and that he gave it to his friend for it to be repaired. He said he never realised the plates got swapped and had no idea how they got there. 

At SCC, the accused provided us with details of a third party who was meant to be fixing the car. These details were given to the informant who could not contact this person.

This matter does not have a reasonable prospect of conviction, as prosecution cannot prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the accused was in possession of the stolen plates at the time. The car appeared abandoned, was unregistered and the accused provided an alibi which we cannot refute if that person gives evidence at court.

Authroisation to withdraw from A/SGT Craig WILLIAMS.
