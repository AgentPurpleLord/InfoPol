## Shaaban bin Hussien v Chong Fook Kam
Privy Council (Malaysia)
07 October 1969
**Abstract:** Reasonable suspicion is less than prima facie proof. Per Lord Devlin. To give power to arrest on reasonable suspicion does not mean that it is always or even ordinarily to be exercised. In countries where common law principles prevail there is no danger in a large measure of executive discretion in the first instance because in those countries the discretion is subject indirectly to judicial control. A piece of timber fell from the trailer of a lorry and broke the windscreen of a car (which was travelling in the opposite direction to the lorry), killing one occupant and injuring another. The lorry did not stop. Early the next day the respondents were questioned by the police, who thought that the respondents were the driver and attendant respectively of the lorry concerned in the accident. At nine a.m. on the next day the respondents were arrested without a warrant or magistrate's order. Such arrest was effected under the Criminal Procedure Code s.23(1)(a) which provided that such an arrest might be effected if the arresting police officer had a reasonable suspicion that the person arrested had been concerned in a seizable offence. During the day, the police checked the respondents' alibis and received unsatisfactory answers which led them to believe the alibis to be false. At six p.m. the respondents were locked up in cells. They were duly brought before a magistrate and ultimately released on the ground that the evidence against against them was insufficient. The Federal Court of Malaysia (reversing the High Court of Malaya) held that the respondents were entitled to damages for false imprisonment in respect of the period from nine a.m. on the day of apprehension until the time of release; the Federal Court held that prima facie proof was required in order to justify an arrest under s.23(1)(a).|

Held, (1) that prima facie proof was not so required; (2) that reasonable suspicion arose only after the alibis had been checked; accordingly (3) that false imprisonment was proved only in respect of the period nine a.m. to six p.m. on the day of apprehension; and (4) that the case be remitted to the Federal Court for a reduction of damages. Decision of Federal Court of Malaysia reversed in part.|

## Section 63 RSA
Clause 63 empowers a member of the police force to enter a vehicle to establish the identity of a driver or motor vehicle or effect an arrest.

Road Safety (Amendment) Bill 2000
Clause 12 amends section 63 of the Road Safety Act 1986 to extend the power of the police to enter a motor vehicle for the purpose of carrying out the provisions of new section 55A, inserted by clause 9, if the driver refuses or fails to obey lawful directions.

Road Safety (Amendment) Bill 2015
Clause 7 amends section 63 of the Road Safety Act 1986 to allow police to enter a motor vehicle for the purposes of carrying out the provisions of new section 55B.

## Director of Public Prosecutions (Vic) v Moreno (2018) 57 VR 1
[125] Section 63 of the Act provides as follows:
“A police officer, or a protective services officer on duty at a designated place, may, for the purpose of establishing the identity of the driver of a motor vehicle or arresting a person or carrying out the provisions of section 53, 54, 55, 55A or 55BA, enter the motor vehicle using, if necessary, reasonable force, if the driver refuses to obey any lawful direction given to him or her by the police officer or the protective services officer. (emphasis added)”
[126] It is noteworthy that while s 63 of the Act enables a police officer to enter a motor vehicle (without the permission of the driver) for the purposes of conducting a preliminary breath test, blood analysis, or drug assessment, the power to enter a vehicle for the purpose of identifying a driver or arresting a person is not confined in any way to the investigation of road safety or driving related offences, or the apprehension of those suspected of committing road safety or driving related offences.
