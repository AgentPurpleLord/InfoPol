- s 53 RSA governs PBT's and it's permitted to be demanded of someone who the police officer finds driving or in charge.
	- _Director of Public Prosecutions v Phillips_ (unreported, VSC, Ashley J, 23 December 1992), p 9. "The need for present and personal observation found in s 53(1)(a) and (2) is to be contrasted with the factual situation contemplated by s 53(1)(c). There, belief as to a past occurrence triggers the right of a police officer to require a preliminary breath test to be undergone."
	- However, the demand by the police demand for a driver to undergo a PBT one and a half hours after he was observed driving was legitimate. There is no requirement under s 53(1)(a) and (2) for the demand to be made forthwith or for the person to remain in the company of the police officer between the time of the officers observation of driving or in charge of and the requirement for the PBT being made. The demand to furnish a PBT sample is lawful if it had been made at any time within three hours of the person being observed driving or in charge of a motor vehicle.
- For the purpose of the EBT requirement, it does not need to be made by the police officer who conducted the PBT or required it, it can be made by *any police officer* (see s 55(1) "any police officer ... may require the person to furnish a sample of breath by a beath analysing instrumment ...")
- BARBARO v SPYROU (1991) 13 MVR 449 Police members in accused’s house when demand for PBT made. Lawful unless members are trespassing.
	- Of significant relevance from that case, at page 2 "The police then heard voices in the house. They walked to the rear door which was open. They then entered the house of their own accord and walked into a kitchen area where they observed the two men they had previously spoken to, two younger males and a middle aged woman ..."
	- They go on to discuss other cases and 'entering a house against a persons will', including specifically, that an open front door IS NOT against their will
	- See page 13 "Further, the two males, when questioned about the whereabouts of the driver of the car, told the police officers that the driver was in the house."
- Fox v Chief Constable of Gwent (1985) 1 WLR 33 was a case where an unlocked but *closed* front door (and officers going through it when no response was heard at their knock) was *not considered to be lawful entry into the house* as they entered as trespassers.
