# Court Supplied Summary
Criminal Law — Evidence — Admissibility — Murder — 000 phone calls by witness — Content — Previous representation — No point of principle — Evidence Act 2008, ss 66, 66A and 137

# Overview
**DPP (Vic) v Henderson (Ruling No 2)**
**[2015] VSC 436 | Supreme Court of Victoria | Justice Bongiorno**

--- 

This ruling concerns the admissibility of two 000 calls made by Rachel Curtis, a key prosecution witness, during a murder incident. The calls were made in highly proximate time and space to the death of the deceased, Timothy O'Brien.

## Issues
1. Admissibility under [[Section 66 Exception—criminal proceedings if maker available]] and [[Section 66A Exception—contemporaneous statements about a person's health etc.]] of the Evidence Act 2008 (Vic).
2. Discretionary exclusions under [[Section 137 Exclusion of prejudicial evidence in criminal proceedings]].

## Key Facts
- Rachel Curtis made two 000 calls: one from the bathroom and one from a paddock.
- The Crown sought to admit the audio and transcripts of these calls as evidence not only of their occurrence but also for the truth of the statements made in them.
- Defence (Ms Boston) opposed the admissibility of the _content_ of Curtis’s calls, arguing she was untruthful and inconsistent.

##  First Ruling (Delivered: 27 June 2015)
Justice Bongiorno:
- Clarified that **s 66 is not concerned with veracity** of the statement, only its **freshness in memory**:
    > “Section 66 is not concerned with the veracity of the prior representation.” [6]

- Acknowledged inconsistencies between Curtis’ courtroom testimony and 000 call statements, but held:
    > “Those inconsistencies, if they exist, may or may not diminish the credit of the witness… but this is a matter for [the jury].” [7]

- Deferred admissibility ruling until after cross-examination:
    > “Accordingly, I will defer ruling on the admissibility of the phone calls until defence counsel has had the opportunity to cross-examine…” [10]


## Second Ruling (Delivered: 4 August 2015)

Following procedural adjustments:
- Curtis was examined on the **voir dire** due to illness and fragmentation concerns.
- Under cross-examination, Curtis admitted some untruths in the calls and uncertainty about some details:
    > “[She] admitted that a number of statements she made to the 000 operator were not true…” [14]  
    > “Her concessions were as to peripheral matters…” [14]

- Defence raised concern over plural pronouns implying multiple attackers and blending of distinct attacks.

Despite these issues, Bongiorno JA ruled:
- The **evidence should be admitted** under **s 137**, referencing _Dupas v The Queen_:
    > “There was no danger here that the jury might fail to recognise Rachael Curtis' untruths… and assess her evidence accordingly.” [16]

- The **jury could weigh the untruths appropriately**, and the **probative value** — including background sounds — outweighed potential prejudice:
    > “The untruths… are not difficult to identify.” [17]  
    > “The probative value… was not outweighed by any danger of any unfair prejudice…” [16]


##  Key Legal Takeaways

- **Section 66** allows for hearsay where the witness is available and the statement is made when the matter was fresh in memory — _truthfulness is not required_ at this threshold.
- **Section 137** requires a practical judgment of whether unreliable evidence might be given _undue weight_. Here, the Court trusted the jury’s capacity to assess credibility given context and volume of other evidence.

## Practical Relevance

This case reinforces that:
- Prior inconsistent statements can still be admissible.
- A witness’s credibility issues don’t necessarily preclude admissibility under s 66.
- Judges will defer to the jury’s role unless there’s a strong risk of unfair prejudice.
