# **Section 51G Crimes Act 1958**
> (1) A person (A) commits an offence if A knowingly possesses child abuse material.
> 
> (2) A person who commits an offence against subsection (1) is liable to **Level 5 Imprisonment (10 years maximum)**.
> 
> (3) For the purposes of subsection (1)  A possessess child abuse material that is electronic material if A controls access to the material whether or not A has physical possession of the electronic material.
>
> (4) It is immaterial that the electronic material was outside Victoria, so long as A was in Victoria at the time at which some or all of the conduct constituting an offence against subsection (1) occurred.
>
> (5) It is immaterial if A was outside Victoria at the time which some or all of the conduct constituting an offence against subsection (1) occurred, so long as the eletronic material was in Victoria. 
>
> **Examples**
>
> 1 A has an online storage account for electronic material accessible with a username and password. A has control of what is stored in the account and can move material around within the account or delete material from the account. A has an electronic folder in the account that A has titled 'personal' in which A puts some electronic child abuse material. A knowingly has possession of child abuse material.
>
> 2  In an online chat A is given a password for a joint email account that is shared with multiple users that A doesn't know. A logs into the email account and views emails that contain child abuse material images. While logged in A has the ability to view, move or delete emails that contain child abuse material. A continues to access the email account to view images. A knowingly has possession of child abuse material.

## Elements of the offence

1. The offence occurred at the **time** and **place** alleged.
2. The offender was the accused.
3. The accused had in his or her possession, child abuse material.
4. That the accused knew that they had in their possession child abuse material.
5. That the accused knew or believed that the material in their possession described or depicted:
   1. a person who is, or who appears or is implied to be, a child as a victim of torture, cruelty or physical abuse; or
   2. in the presence of another person who is engaged in, or apparently engaged in, a sexual pose or sexual activity; or
   3. the genital or anal region of a person who is, or who appears or is implied to be, a child; or
   4. the breast area of a person who is or who appears to be or is implied to be a female child;
6. And reasonable person would regard as being, in the circumstances, offensive.

## Cache Files or 'Deleted' Files

From Littlejohn v Hamilton (2010) 199 A Crim R 63 at [17]:

- the prosecution would need to establish knowledge that a deleted image remained on the hard drive and was capable of being retrieved;
- possession in the sense of physical custody or control cannot exist where the person does not, at the relevant time, have the means to retrieve or gain access to a deleted image;
- accordingly, even where a person knows of the existence of a deleted image on the hard drive and that there are means by which that can be accessed, without having those means there is no actual possession;
- whether the means are available to the particular defendant is a question of fact.

## Knowledge of possession

The fourth and fifth elements require that the accused "knowingly possessed" child abuse material. It merely being on a device is not enough to satisfy this element.

### R v Campbell (2009) 195 A Crim R 374

Offences Against Decency and Morality — Physical elements — Possession — Child exploitation material — Character in fictional story — Whether ‘knowingly possessed’ — Whether unsafe verdict — Criminal Code (Qld), s 228D

- The accused was charged under Section 228D of the Criminal Code (Qld) for "knowingly possessing child exploitation material."
- On appeal, defence argued that the trial Judge did not sufficiently direct the jury on the law relating to possession.
- The forensic investigation officer who reviewed the computers gave evidence.
  - He found two copies of child abuse material in separate locations. One directly on the C: drive in a folder called "Assignment." Another in a folder called "JLC Assignment." (see [19])
  - The files were created on 26 January 2006 and modified on 7 September 2005.
- The officers from search warrant said that when they disconnected the computer from an uninterruptable power supply, the computer had an alarm go off.
- It became apparent over the course of the trial *three* CAM documents existed.
  - 'Document C' which was a hard-copy of the CAM, printed by the accused's wife and given to a psychologist. This was ultimately given to police and tendered.
  - Two electronic versions, each within their different sub-folders within the "Assignments" folder.
- At [53] the point is made that it is incredibly important with CAM matters that the *specific* material is shown in evidence to be linked to the *specific* charge laid for it.
- Document C was never shown to be in the possession of the accused, as the wife printed it and gave it to the psychologist. 
- R v Shew was compared with this case.
  - R v Shew contained "admissions from the appellant" and "admissions to the ownership of the computer." 
  - This case did not. 
- On that basis, the appeal was granted, conviction quashed and a not guilty verdict entered.

> [11] "Now, the evidence you'll hear is that admissions were made and the document was found on a computer in Mr Campbell's house. The prosecution case is that Mr Campbell, the defendant, downloaded the document from the internet at some point of time and that he had possession of it in an electronic format and that when the police searched his house on the 22nd of March 2006 they found two copies of that document. One copy of the document on the face of it, the computer expert will tell you, was downloaded from the internet on the 7th of September 2005 and then manually saved to that computer and that a second copy had been saved from that original copy also onto the computer and it was an identical document that hadn't been re-downloaded from the internet but rather copied across from that original file.”
>
> [65] As in Shew's case, the evidence in the present case was in short compass. But in contrast to Shew's case, there were no admissions by the appellant with respect to either the hard copy of the document or either of the computer files, nor any admissions even concerning ownership of the computer, nor any evidence from any other person as to the provenance of the hard copy document or ownership of the black tower computer, such as to found the sorts of inferences to which reference was made in Shew's case. On the contrary, and for the reasons I have given above, I consider there was not, as a matter of law, sufficient evidence to sustain the verdict.

### [R v Shew [1998] QCA 333](https://classic.austlii.edu.au/au/cases/qld/QCA/1998/333.html)

CRIMINAL LAW - "Knowingly" having possession of child abuse computer game - unsafe and unsatisfactory conviction - Failure of accused to give evidence.

- On 11 December 1997, the accused was convicted of "knowingly possessing a child abuse computer game" which was an offence under s 26(3) of the Classification of Computer Games and Images Act 1995 (Qld).
- On 26 December 1996, the accused's wife gave police 37 computer disks and told them they had child pornography on them.
- The accused denied knowing what was on the disks when interviewed. He raised relationship issues with his wife and said he thought his wife set him up.
- The accused was shown the disks and he was shown a beige/grey plastic container which contained 11 disks. The accused admitted that the 11 in that box were his, but denied any child pornography being on them.
- The disks were analysed. The files said they were created on 19 December 1996.
- The case was one in which guilty intent could only be infered from other circumstances (see [20]).

>  [22] The appellant's admission that the disks were owned by him appears to be based simply on his acceptance that there were disks in the container which he acknowledged was his property. In most circumstances, it would be open to infer that the owner of the box, particularly if it had been in his exclusive possession, might know what its contents were. But here the applicant did not have exclusive possession and his admission as to its contents came with the qualification that there was no child pornography on the disks. Despite that it remained open to the jury to infer that the box and its contents belonged to the appellant and at the same time to reject his assertion that there was no offending material on them. The appellant himself made a distinction between the disks in the box and the ones which were loose.
>
> [28] The element of "knowingly" is a matter which the jury was entitled to infer beyond reasonable doubt from the fact that the creation of the files constituting the child abuse computer game occurred at a time when the applicant was making admissions that he was actively dealing with the computer disks and prior to his admission of possession of them. The jury of course were not bound to draw the inference they did. A different jury may not have drawn the same inference but so far as the learned trial judge was concerned his approach was to have regard to the remarks of the High Court in Doney v. The Queen [1990] HCA 51; (1990) 171 C.L.R. 207 (at p.214) -
> >
> > "But it is appropriate here to draw attention to the fact that the drawing of inferences extends beyond circumstantial evidence because the purpose and the genius of the jury system is that it allows for the ordinary experiences of ordinary people to be brought to bear in the determination of factual matters. It is fundamental to that purpose that the jury be allowed to determine, by inference from its collective experience of ordinary affairs, whether and, in the case of conflict, what evidence is truthful."
>
> [39] In this case the evidence was in short compass. With so little challenge being made to the evidence, the jury's advantage in hearing and seeing the witnesses was not of much moment. Once one reaches the point that there was sufficient evidence upon which it was open to the jury to draw an inference of guilt then this Court is being asked really to draw inferences different to those drawn by the jury. If the jury has accepted, as we perceive they did, that the electronic files containing the offending material were created on Thursday 19 December 1996, that the appellant admitted that he handled the files on Friday 20 December 1996 and that he was prepared to accept ownership of the computer disks which were in his computer box knowing the allegation they contained child pornography there is a basis upon which the inference could be drawn that he knowingly had possession of that material.
>
> [40] It was open to the jury to be satisfied beyond reasonable doubt that the accused was guilty.

### Littlejohn v Hamilton (2010) 199 A Crim R 63

Offences Against Decency and Morality — Possession of child pornography and bestiality product — Fault elements — Knowledge of presence of images on computer — Evidence of access to computer by others — Evidence of large-scale downloads — Retrievability of deleted images — Whether possession proved — Classification (Publications, Films and Computer Games) Enforcement Act 1995 (Tas), s 74(a), (b)

- Accused found guilty of possessing child abuse product and beastility product contrary to Classification (Publications, Films and Computer Games) Enforcement Act 1995 (Tas), s 74(a) and (b).
- CAM: 2,500 images, 100 videos.
- On appeal it was argued:
  - The illegal images had been trasmitted onto the accused's computer without his knowledge.
  - That the evidence did not establish that the images had been deleted and that they could only be access with specialist computer equipment.
- On 30 June 2005, a search warrant was executed at the accused's home. A computer tower, external hard drive and floppy disks were seized. A total of four hard-drives, each with three partitions were discovered amongst thos exhibits.
  - There was a CD amongst the exhibits which contained copied files located on the hard drives.
- The Forensic Analyst gave evidence:
  - The 'last access' date could indicate a date when an anti-virus program scanned the file, but could also indicate when someone used the file.
  - Not all of the materials were in one folder, but in numerous throughout the hard-drives. They were within folders titlted 'porn' and 'no porn in here' or female names.
  - The internet history and favourites saved on the hard drives contained a large number of pornographic websites, some relating to young females.
  - That if a person allowed another person access over a local area network connection, they might be able to add or delete files onto another computer, e.g. file transfer and file leeching/torrenting.
  - The forensic report would indicate which images had been deleted or which remained on the hard drive in their original form.
- The accused gave evidence:
  - He said he went to a LAN party in Launceston that went for 2 days and that he had leeched a substantial amount of data (400gb).
  - Whilst at the LAN party, one of the administrators messaged him "Dude that kind of shit will get you kicked out of here." When clarified, the administrator said "kiddie porn will get you kicked out of the LAN."
  - The accused then checked the image in question which was titled "14 year old lesbian", but that the woman in that image appeared well over 18.
  - He told the administrator he had leeched that file, but that he would delete it and did so.
  - He put his case that he had not knowingly and intentionally put the CAM on his computer and had never found the files.
- The following important four points relating to possession of deleted images were made in this case at [17]:
  - the prosecution must establish knowledge that a deleted image remained on the hard drive and could be retrivevd.
  - possession cannot exist where the person does not, at the relevant time, have the means to retrieve or gain access to a deleted image.
  - even when a person knows of the existence of a deleted image and that there are means of accessing it, without having those means it is not possession.
  - whether the means are available to the defendant is a question of fact.
- Three particular facts in issue were identified in this case:
  - the meaning of the "file created" date; and
  - the meaning of the "file modification" date; and
  - the location of relevant images on the hard drive and whether they had been deleted.
- In dealing with these three key points, the following evidence was in favour of the prosecution:
  - A variety of file creation dates over various months, which went against the account of the LAN party over 2 days.
  - File modification dates occuring in bursts to batches of images, typically in the early morning or afternoon.
  - That some files were deleted but others were not.
  - Files were found over numerous partitions in different folders and on different hard drives.
  - The accused admitted that if an anti-virus software had scanned the files, all the files on  the whole computer would have the same file modified date.
  - The accused admitted that if the files were shared, it would say who had shared them, which was inconsistent with his evidence about anonymous sharing.
  - The accused would not agree his computer was organised, only that it was organised 'in some fashion.'
  - When directed to the favourites folder, the accused was evasive and said he didn't know how they got there.
  - The accused similarly could not explain his access to websites a few days before the seizure.

> [46] Lastly, at least as to the first count on the complaint, the submission made to the magistrate and repeated in this Court as to an alternative basis for conviction has much merit, although I do not need to formally determine it. I think that the magistrate could have been well satisfied beyond reasonable doubt that all the relevant images were intentionally possessed by the applicant on 28 May 2005, the day on which they were modified. The magistrate did not see a need to deal with this submission either, but if there be any doubt as to the integrity of the findings of guilt as to possession on 30 June 2005, there can be no such difficulties with findings of intentional possession of relevant images on 28 May 2005. The variance between the date alleged in the complaint of 30 June 2005, and 28 May 2005, fell within the provisions of the Justices Act 1959 (Tas), s 31(1)(b), and the magistrate would have been entitled to convict on that basis.
