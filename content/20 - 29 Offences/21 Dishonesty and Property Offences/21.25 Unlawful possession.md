---
title: 21.25 Unlawful possession
date: 2026-03-11
---


>[!INFO] Summary Offences Act 1966 - Section 26 
>(1) Any person having in his actual possession or conveying in any manner any personal property whatsoever reasonably suspected of being stolen or unlawfully obtained whether in or outside Victoria may be arrested either with or without warrant and brought before a bail justice or the Magistrates' Court, or may be summoned to appear before the Magistrates' Court.
>
>(2) If such person does not in the opinion of the court give a satisfactory account as to how he came by such property he shall be guilty of an offence. 
>
>Penalty: Imprisonment for one year.
>
>(3) Upon proof that any property was or had been in the actual possession of such person or under his control and whether or not such person still has possession or control thereof when brought before the court the property shall for the purposes of this section be deemed to be in his actual possession.
>
>(4) Where a person is charged before the Magistrates' Court with an offence under this section the court may proceed to hear and determine the matter notwithstanding that it appears from the evidence that the person charged stole or unlawfully obtained the property concerned in a place outside Victoria in circumstances amounting to the commission of a criminal offence in that place.


# Defences

- A belief they had a right to deprive the item.
- A belief they had consent to deprive the item.
- The owner could not be located taking reasonable steps.

# Points of Proof
1. The offence occurred at the *place* and *time* alleged.
2. The offender *was the accused*. 
3. The accused had in his/her *possession* or was *conveying* in any manner any *personal property*.
4. The property was *reasonably suspected of being stolen* or *unlawfully obtained*.
5. The *accused* did not give to the court a *satisfactory account* of how he/she came by the property.

## Actual possession and conveying
In [_Moors v Burke_ (1919) 26 CLR 265](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U2&serNum=1919034198&pubNum=0003586&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&ppcid=35765fe668334b5fbf9622d5badd4f6c&contextData=\(sc.Keycite\)) at 274 the court said:

> “Having actual possession” means, in this enactment, simply having at the time, in actual fact and without the necessity of taking any further step, the complete present personal physical control of the property to the exclusion of others not acting in concert with the accused, and whether he has that control by having the property in his present manual custody, or by having it where he alone has the exclusive right or power to place his hands on it, and so have manual custody when he wishes. In its nature it corresponds to its companion expression “conveying” which necessarily involves instant personal physical control to the exclusion of others. These two expressions are obviously intended to cover the whole ground of actual personal control – that is, whether the property is kept stationary or is in motion. But it does not include the case of a person who has put the property out of his present manual custody and deposited it in a place where any other person independently of him has an equal right and power of getting it, and so may prevent the first from ever getting manual custody in the future. In that event the property is not in his actual possession: it is where he may possibly reduce it again into actual possession, or, on the other hand, where the other person may himself reduce it into his own actual exclusive possession.

## Reasonably suspected to be stolen
The suspicion must be that the property is obtained in a manner analogous to theft. A suspicion it is derived from some other illegal act such as drug trafficking will not suffice: [_Nicholls v Young_ [1992] 2 VR 209 at 214](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U2&serNum=1992357743&pubNum=0004888&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&ppcid=35765fe668334b5fbf9622d5badd4f6c&contextData=\(sc.Keycite\)#co_pp_214).

The suspicion must also be contemporaneous with the possession: [_Nicholls v Young_ [1992] 2 VR 209 at 215](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U2&serNum=1992357743&pubNum=0004888&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&ppcid=35765fe668334b5fbf9622d5badd4f6c&contextData=\(sc.Keycite\)#co_pp_215); [_Rowe v Galvin_ [1984] VR 350](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U2&serNum=1984183050&pubNum=0004888&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&ppcid=35765fe668334b5fbf9622d5badd4f6c&contextData=\(sc.Keycite\)). The effect of [_Summary Offences Act 1966_ s 26(3)](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U5&docFamilyGuid=I3356a285510611e9ba47b982d234a9a6&pubNum=1100190&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&docVersion=Law+in+Force&ppcid=35765fe668334b5fbf9622d5badd4f6c&contextData=\(sc.Keycite\)) is to provide that where there is proof that at some time before the accused is brought before the court he or she was in actual possession of property and it was reasonably and contemporaneously suspected of being stolen, the offence may be established despite the accused not being in actual possession at the time the charge is determined: [_Brown v Schiffman_ [1911] VLR 133 at 135](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U2&serNum=1911038562&pubNum=0004887&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&ppcid=35765fe668334b5fbf9622d5badd4f6c&contextData=\(sc.Keycite\)#co_pp_135); [_Director of Public Prosecutions v Pastras_ (2005) 11 VR 449 at 453–454](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U2&serNum=2006362982&pubNum=0004888&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&ppcid=35765fe668334b5fbf9622d5badd4f6c&contextData=\(sc.Keycite\)#co_pp_453).

The suspicion must attach to the property, and not to the accused: [_O'Sullivan v Tregaskis_ [1948] SASR 12](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U2&serNum=1948026117&pubNum=0004840&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&ppcid=35765fe668334b5fbf9622d5badd4f6c&contextData=\(sc.Keycite\)); [_Yeo v Capper_ [1964] SASR 1](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U2&serNum=1964056146&pubNum=0004840&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&ppcid=35765fe668334b5fbf9622d5badd4f6c&contextData=\(sc.Keycite\)).

Of course, the requirement for reasonable suspicion imports the need for the suspicion to be based upon reasonable grounds: see [[79.130]](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?findType=L&pubNum=0234476&sourceSerial=%5b79.130%5d&originatingDoc=I9136bae50cc011ec8c5aae4ce6053189&refType=PY&fi=co_pp_sp_234476_%5b79.130%5d&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&ppcid=35765fe668334b5fbf9622d5badd4f6c&contextData=\(sc.Keycite\)#co_pp_sp_234476_[79.130]).

In _Patten v Adams_,[96](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Document/I180299900b3f11ea83b0ac48dfa2554a/View/FullText.html?navigationPath=%2FRelatedInfo%2Fv1%2FkcCitingReferences%2Fnav%3FdocGuid%3DI4faccdb8035111e99495db3043f758b0%26midlineIndex%3D7%26warningFlag%3DB%26planIcons%3DYES%26skipOutOfPlan%3DYES%26sort%3Dtreatmentasc%26filterGuid%3Dh41554ea1965e1d2fac01b1e102fb72a7%26category%3DkcCitingReferences%26origDocSource%3D540cfd3bd9064552afc6e88d7331b56c&listSource=RelatedInfo&list=CitingReferences&rank=7&docFamilyGuid=I07998c100b3f11ea83b0ac48dfa2554a&ppcid=bb8c9aefef954c0fb4f2bd6249b99633&originationContext=citingreferences&transitionType=CitingReferencesItem&contextData=\(sc.DocLink\)&comp=wlau#co_footnote_96) the defendant was charged with being in possession of gold in respect of which he was unable to furnish receipts. The defendant further gave inconsistent and contradictory answers in respect of the gold and its provenance. It was held that on a charge under [s 26](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U7&docFamilyGuid=I3356a285510611e9ba47b982d234a9a6&pubNum=1100190&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&docVersion=Law+in+Force&ppcid=9ad86642843b446e903fad05c5db1f98&contextData=\(sc.Keycite\)), the onus is on the prosecution to bring evidence of a reasonably-based suspicion that property was stolen or unlawfully obtained. If the court determines that the suspicion was reasonable, the defendant is guilty of the offence unless it is established (on balance of probabilities) that possession of the property was legally obtained.

## Satisfactory account
The heart of the offence contrary to [_Summary Offences Act 1966_ s 26](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U5&docFamilyGuid=I3356a285510611e9ba47b982d234a9a6&pubNum=1100190&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&docVersion=Law+in+Force&ppcid=35765fe668334b5fbf9622d5badd4f6c&contextData=\(sc.Keycite\)) is of failing to give satisfactory account of being in possession of the goods, once the prosecution establishes beyond reasonable doubt the goods were reasonably suspected of being stolen: [_Willis v Burnes_ (1921) 29 CLR 511 at 514](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U2&serNum=1921018147&pubNum=0003586&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&ppcid=35765fe668334b5fbf9622d5badd4f6c&contextData=\(sc.Keycite\)#co_pp_514) per Knox CJ.

The accused has the legal onus of establishing a satisfactory account under [s 26(2)](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U7&docFamilyGuid=I3356a285510611e9ba47b982d234a9a6&pubNum=1100190&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&docVersion=Law+in+Force&ppcid=35765fe668334b5fbf9622d5badd4f6c&contextData=\(sc.Keycite\)): see [[4.20]](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?findType=L&pubNum=0234476&sourceSerial=%5b4.20%5d&originatingDoc=I9136bae50cc011ec8c5aae4ce6053189&refType=PY&fi=co_pp_sp_234476_%5b4.20%5d&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&ppcid=35765fe668334b5fbf9622d5badd4f6c&contextData=\(sc.Keycite\)#co_pp_sp_234476_[4.20]).

# Charge
The accused at [place] on [date] had in [his/her] actual possession, personal property, namely [description of property], reasonably suspected of being stolen or unlawfully obtained.
