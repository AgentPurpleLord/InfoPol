>Road Safety Road Rules 2017
>Rule 126 Keeping a safe distance behind vehicles
>A driver must drive a sufficient distance behind a vehicle travelling in front of the driver so the driver can, if necessary, stop safely to avoid a collision with the vehicle.
>Penalty: In the case of a driver of a large vehicle, 10 penalty units; In any other case, 5 penalty units

A police officer gave evidence that, whilst travelling at approximately 55 km/h, he observed a vehicle behind him in his rear-view mirror that appeared very close. He was not able to see the headlights or bonnet of the vehicle. His fellow officer gave an estimate that the following vehicle was two to five m behind. The first officer then commenced to slow his vehicle before ultimately coming to a complete stop during which time the following vehicle continued to remain very close to the police vehicle. When he came to a complete stop the following vehicle overtook. The evidence of the officer was that, if he had to brake suddenly, the following vehicle would have hit the back of the police vehicle. Held, on appeal by the driver against the conviction at first instance, the evidence was capable of proving beyond reasonable doubt that the driver had not driven a safe distance behind the police car: _Yuan v Police_ [2012] SASC 221.

The rule arose for consideration in a negligence case where a bus passenger was injured after being thrown from her seat when the bus driver braked heavily to avoid a collision with a car in front of the bus which had come to an abrupt stop. Held, interpretation of the rule is informed by two considerations. First, it is clear that the rule is directed towards the prevention of a collision in the event that, for whatever reason, a vehicle stops in front of a driver who is following it too closely. The rule must be read as a whole in the light of that purpose. Secondly, the word “safely” must be given some operation. A driver would clearly be in breach of r 126 (of the 2008 Rules, the predecessor of r 126 of the 2014 Rules) if he or she were travelling behind another vehicle at such a distance that the only manner of stopping to avoid a collision would endanger other road users or pedestrians. For instance, it would be a breach of the rule for a driver to follow so closely behind another vehicle that he or she could only stop, if necessary, by braking in a manner which would result in loss of control of the vehicle. Tailgating would be another example. However, the rule should not be read as imposing so high a standard as to render it an offence, without more, to stop suddenly, successfully avoiding a collision, but in doing so causing a passenger in the braking vehicle to be injured. That a driver does so gives rise to no necessary inference, in the absence of evidence of the particular circumstances which gave rise to the necessity to avoid a collision, that there was a breach of the rule: _Penrith City Council v East Realisations Pty Ltd (in liq)_ [2013] NSWCA 64.

A driver is obliged to allow enough room so that the vehicle could be brought to a stop even if the driver in front suddenly braked and came to a halt due to some unexpected hazard (in this case to avoid hitting an animal on the road): _Howard-Hill v James_ (2018) 85 MVR 490; [2018] ACTSC 249.

A driver is to keep such sufficient distance behind a vehicle travelling in front so that the driver can, if necessary, stop safely to avoid collision with that vehicle. This requires a driver to be keeping a proper lookout and to adjust speed when necessary: _Collins v Insurance Australia Ltd_ [2021] NSWDC 371. (NB – the decision was overturned on appeal with the Court of Appeal reaching different conclusions regarding the driver's actions: _Collins v Insurance Australia Ltd_ [2022] NSWCA 135.)
