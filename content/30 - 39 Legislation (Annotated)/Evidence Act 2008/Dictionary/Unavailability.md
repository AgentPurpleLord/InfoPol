Found within Evidence Act 2008 - Part 1 - Definitions - Clause 4
> 1. For the purposes of this Act, a person is taken not to be available to give evidence about a fact if-
> 	1. the person is dead; or
> 	2. the person is, for any reason other than the application of section 16 (Competence and compellability—judges and jurors), not competent to give the evidence; or
> 	3. the person is mentally or physically unable to give the evidence and it is not reasonably practicable to overcome that inability; or
> 	4. it would be unlawful for the person to give the evidence; or
> 	5. a provision of this Act prohibits the evidence being given; or
> 	6. all reasonable steps have been taken, by the party seeking to prove the person is not available, to find the person or secure his or her attendance, but without success; or
> 	7. all reasonable steps have been taken, by the party seeking to prove the person is not available, to compel the person to give the evidence, but without success.
> 2. In all other cases the person is taken to be available to give evidence about the fact.

# Judicial College Notes
1. A witness who is not compellable to give particular evidence meets the definition in paragraph (f). This includes a witness who successfully applies to be excused under s18 (_DPP v Nicholls_ [2010] VSC 397. See also _Fletcher v R_ (2015) 45 VR 634; [2015] VSCA 146).
2. Similarly, a witness who is called and refuses to give evidence, whether through an exercise of privilege or under threat of contempt, is not available under paragraph (f) (_R v Suteski_ (2002) 56 NSWLR 182; [2002] NSWCCA 509; _R v Alchin_ [2006] ACTSC 53; _R v Darmody_ (2010) 25 VR 209; [2010] VSCA 41; _Mindshare Communications Ltd Taiwan Branch v Orleans Investments Pty Ltd_ [2007] NSWSC 976).
3. Paragraphs (e) and (f) require the party to have taken ‘all reasonable steps’. This must be applied strictly before the court will take the significant step of considering a witness unavailable. Relevant considerations will include whether the party took timely steps to secure the witness’ attendance, especially where the party was on notice that the witness may not attend (_Sio v R_ (2016) 259 CLR 47; [2016] HCA 32; _Tasmania v Dolega_ [2016] TASSC 65 at [12]-[15]).
4. Reasonable steps, for the purpose of paragraph (f), will usually involve issuing a subpoena to attend. The fact that the witness, or their guardian, states they will resist, or not comply with, a subpoena does not mean that a subpoena is not necessary as a ‘reasonable step’ (_Clancy v Plaintiff A & Ors_ [2022] NSWCA 119, [69]).
5. Similarly, the fact that giving evidence may be detrimental to a witness’ health does not make the witness unavailable within the meaning of the Act. Such a risk is one to be managed by the court after the subpoena has been issued, such as in an application to set the subpoena aside (_Clancy v Plaintiff A & Ors_ [2022] NSWCA 119, [70]).
6. For the purpose of paragraph (g), reasonable steps may include the provision of a witness assistance officer, an out of court conversation between counsel and the witness to try to overcome the witness’ reluctance, an application for the court to warn the witness of the possibility of contempt and an application for an adjournment so the witness can obtain legal advice about possibility of contempt proceedings (_RC v The Queen_ [2022] NSWCCA 281, [119]).

# Uniform Evidence Law Commentary
## (1)(c) the person is mentally or physically unable to give the evidence and it is not reasonably practicable to overcome that inability; or
- It was proposed in ALRC 102, paragraphs 8.34 to 8.35.
[T]he Commissions have retained the formula “mentally or physically unable”. To prevent abuse of the amendment and to prevent the amendment being applied to discriminate against persons wrongly, the proposed amendment now contains a qualification that the “inability” of the witness “cannot reasonably be overcome”. This is designed to exclude the possibility that (for example) a person unable to speak or hear but who can communicate in writing may be considered “physically unable” to testify: there will generally be reasonable measures for overcoming such difficulties.
As to mental inability, it is intended that such an amendment may facilitate, in at least some cases, the admission of the transcript of a complainant’s evidence in a retrial. Requiring the complainant to testify again may, depending on the circumstances of the case, do such emotional or psychological harm to the complainant that the complainant should be considered unavailable to give the particular evidence.
- Emotional or psychological harm may be caused and so that witness would be regarded as unable to give their evidence. 
- R v Nona (2015) 254 A Crim R 301; [2015] ACTSC 175, Refshauge J of ACT Supreme Court.
	- The test would not be satisfied simply on the basis that the witness had memory problems (at 141).
	- There would need to be a condition which caused memory loss or amnesia (at 142).

## (1)(f)  all reasonable steps have been taken, by the party seeking to prove the person is not available, to find the person or secure his or her attendance, but without success; or
- This is *not* satisfied if the party has not taken all reasoanble steps to secure attendance.
- In Caterpillar Inc v John Deere Ltd (No 2) (2000) 181 ALR 108; witness was living in United States and it did not seem appealing to attend trial in Australia. The party calling this witness *failed* to take all reasonable steps as they did not establish personal contact and did not offer to pay expenses (at 17, 18 and 19).
- In Seafood Imports Pty Ltd v ANL Singapore Pte Ltd (No 1) [2009] FCA 435 held; an asumption or intuitation that a person is in Japan would not co-operate in being called to give evidence via video link did not discharge the onus imposed to show unavailability.
- In R v Sarbandi (2012) 229 A Crime R 39; a person in Saudi Arabia had not been offered financial assistance to come to Australia, and it cannot be assumed that such a person understood this could be provided.
- In Tim Barr Pty Ltd v Narui Gold Coast Pty Ltd (2009) 258 ALR 598
	-  A person was in Japan and stated that he "had no intention of giving evidence in the future, either in person or by video link."
	- Pursuant to the Act, it was concluded that this meant all reasonable steps were taken to secure his attendance but without success.

## (1)(g) all reasonable steps have been taken, by the party seeking to prove the person is not available, to compel the person to give the evidence, but without success.
The issue of securing the attendance of a witness was considered by Heerey J in [_Caterpillar Inc v John Deere Ltd (No 2)_ [2000] FCA 1903](http://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?findType=Y&serNum=2001045943&pubNum=0006270&refType=U2&originationContext=ebook&RS=ebbp3.0&vr=3.0&comp=wlau). He concluded that the party had not taken all reasonable steps to compel the person's attendance within the meaning of cl 4(1)(g) (at [20]). No attempt had been made to take advantage of s 7 of the _Foreign Evidence Act 1974_ (Cth). The party could not merely assert that because the person was a United States resident he was beyond the process of the Federal Court and could not be compelled to give evidence. However, the steps to be taken are all of those that are reasonable, not all of those that are conceivably possible.6

As regards paragraph (g), in [_Mindshare Communications Ltd v Orleans Investment Pty Ltd_ [2007] NSWSC 976](http://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?findType=Y&serNum=2013147314&pubNum=0006084&refType=U2&originationContext=ebook&RS=ebbp3.0&vr=3.0&comp=wlau), Hamilton J accepted (at [14]–[18]) that the purpose of the provision:

> is to deal with [the] situation where the attendance of the witness has been secured, but it is impossible to obtain the evidence, because, for instance, the witness declines to give it on the ground of privilege or simply refuses to give it, whatever threats are made concerning the consequences arising out of a contempt of court.7

Whether “all reasonable steps” have been taken will depend on the particular circumstances of the case.8 The mere fact that a witness refuses to answer questions, without more, will not always satisfy the requirements of cl 4(g). There must be some evidence that “all reasonable steps” have been taken to compel the witness to give evidence.9 Some relevant considerations include the nature of the case, the importance of the evidence, the applicable standard of proof and the importance of the liberty of the individual.10 Where a witness has been called and refuses to testify, notwithstanding a threat of contempt proceedings, the requirements of the paragraph will usually be satisfied.11 The same is true of a (potential) witness who simply refuses to enter the court or the witness box.12 However, where the witness is not warned about the possibility of contempt proceedings, they may not be satisfied.13 The requirements will be satisfied where a witness is not required to give evidence pursuant to s 18.14 Where the witness is an “associated defendant” for the purposes of s 17, “reasonable steps” might include a request that the associated defendant consent to giving evidence in the course of the joint trial pursuant to s 17(4) or an application to sever the trials and to compel the co-accused to give evidence pursuant to s 17(3) in the separate trial.15 The situation will be more complex where the witness indicates a willingness to testify at some time in the future.16 In [_Tan v The Queen_ (2008) 192 A Crim R 310; [2008] NSWCCA 332](http://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?findType=Y&serNum=2018310429&pubNum=0005341&refType=U2&originationContext=ebook&RS=ebbp3.0&vr=3.0&comp=wlau), the NSW Court of Criminal Appeal held that the test would not be satisfied where “a person enters the witness box, is prepared to respond to questions but does so in an allegedly untruthful manner by claiming lack of recollection or loss of memory (not due to s 13 incompetency)”: Tobias JA at [57], Barr J and Kirby J agreeing. Tobias JA considered such circumstances “more aptly fall within s 38(1)(b)” (the unfavourable witness provision), so that the person could be questioned as if in cross-examination by the party calling him (and any prior inconsistent statement of the witness may be admissible pursuant to Pt 3.7). It is apparent that Tobias JA was influenced by the fact that the application of cl 4(1)(f) arose in the context of the operation of the exceptions to the hearsay rule in s 65(2). He accepted at [55] a submission that ss 38 and 65 were “mutually exclusive”. This analysis is not entirely persuasive. This clause is not limited in its application to s 65. In any event, it is difficult to see why ss 38 and 65 should be regarded as mutually exclusive. They deal with different issues, the first with the manner in which a witness may be questioned, the second with whether a previous representation is admissible notwithstanding the hearsay rule. Nevertheless the ruling appears correct, for different reasons. If the words of cl 4(1)(g) are given their ordinary meaning, it is difficult to see how it can be said that a person has failed to give “evidence about” the alleged making of a previous representation by him when he testifies that he does not have any memory of making it (or, indeed, denies making it), even if the court is satisfied that the testimony is untruthful.

RC v The King[2022] NSWCCA 281
Para 3: The non-cooperation of the complainant, CG, presented the trial judge and both counsel with difficult decisions, which had to be made under the pressure of the applicant being in the jury's charge and CG misconducting herself and issuing a stream of vile abuse towards the prosecutor and the judge. CG was called to support allegations that she had made some three and a half years before the trial. Upon those allegations police had conducted an investigation and delivered a brief to the Director of Public Prosecutions. Having thus activated the machinery of the law, by the time the trial was conducted CG had changed her mind. When called as a witness she addressed the Crown prosecutor and the trial judge in foul language and, for the most part, refused to give responsive answers to questions. The Crown prosecutor might well have applied for further steps to be taken to compel the witness. He might have sought to have her counselled and advised as a means of encouraging her to give evidence. The learned judge might have adjourned to facilitate such a course before receiving CG's police statement into evidence. The statement having been admitted, defence counsel might have cross-examined on it. None of the participants can be criticised for not having found, under difficult circumstances, a solution that might have enabled the trial to proceed to sound verdicts. CG sabotaged the proceeding.
Para 8: For the purposes of [s 65(2)(c) of the Evidence Act](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U7&docFamilyGuid=I1b61194f40b411e99c46ebfdb1e1f82b&pubNum=1100190&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&docVersion=Law+in+Force&ppcid=39c41d1cb24840649e9cd73b82ca9c3d&contextData=\(sc.Default\)), I agree with Yehia J that it would have been open to the trial judge to find that CG's police statement was made in such circumstances that it was highly likely to be reliable. However, CG was not “unavailable” so that [s 65](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U7&docFamilyGuid=I1b61194f40b411e99c46ebfdb1e1f82b&pubNum=1100190&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&docVersion=Law+in+Force&ppcid=39c41d1cb24840649e9cd73b82ca9c3d&contextData=\(sc.Default\)) was not engaged. It was not shown that all reasonable steps had been taken to compel CG to give evidence, as required in order to satisfy cl 4(1)(g) of the definition of an unavailable person, according to the Dictionary to the [Evidence Act](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U7&docFamilyGuid=Ia65f9d05f8f511e8bc3e94cdd1e3c560&pubNum=1100190&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&docVersion=Law+in+Force&ppcid=39c41d1cb24840649e9cd73b82ca9c3d&contextData=\(sc.Default\)). If further steps to compel CG had been taken, without success, so that she fulfilled the definition of an unavailable witness, enabling her statement to be tendered under [s 65](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U7&docFamilyGuid=I1b61194f40b411e99c46ebfdb1e1f82b&pubNum=1100190&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&docVersion=Law+in+Force&ppcid=39c41d1cb24840649e9cd73b82ca9c3d&contextData=\(sc.Default\)), consideration would have to have been given to whether the danger of unfair prejudice to the accused as a result of receiving the statement would outweigh its probative value. The danger would have been very significant in view of the apparent impossibility of cross-examining CG because of her uncooperative conduct.
