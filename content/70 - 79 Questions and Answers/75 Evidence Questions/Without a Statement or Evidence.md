>[!QUESTION] Without any statement, or a lack of evidence, can I arrest and interview a person?

>[!INFO] The Short Answer (tldr)
>Yes, but tread carefully.
>
>You must have enough information (whether in the form of a jurated statement or otherwise) to form your belief that the offence was committed.  


This is a legally complex question, but, is found semi-regularly in a uniform policing setting. Often you will attend an incident where you identify involved parties and identify an offence, but your victim doesn't want to sign a statement or the matter to proceed to court.

Some ere on the side of caution; they won't arrest the suspect. Others always arrest the suspect which has led to criticism about illegal arrests.

In this question, we examine the fundamental of performing an investigation, which is typically broken down into 3 steps:
1. **Information Gathering** - police gather information from parties, no one is under caution, we are attempting to ascertain if an offence has been committed.
2. **Identifying Accused** - we have enough information to have a suspect, but not to believe they are the person who committed the offence. We typically caution the person and seek admissions or search for further evidence.
3. **Charging the Accused** - Arresting, interviewing and consequently charging a suspect with the matter, as we have formed a belief on reasonable grounds that the suspect committed an indictable offence.

Lawton LJ in R v Osbourne [1972] EWCA Crim J1208-2 put this best:
>The rules contemplate three stages in the investigations leading up to somebody being brought before a court for a criminal offence. _The first is the gathering of information, and that can be gathered from anybody_, including persons in custody provided they have not been charged. At the gathering of information stage no caution of any kind need be administered. The final stage, the one contemplated by rule III of the Judges' Rules, is when the police officer has got enough (and I stress the word "enough") evidence to prefer a charge. That is clear from the introduction to the Judges' Rules which sets out the principle. But a police officer when carrying out an investigation meets a stage in between the mere gathering of information and the getting of enough evidence to prefer the charge. _He reaches a stage where he has got the beginnings of evidence. It is at that stage that he must caution._ In the judgment of this court, he is not bound to caution until he has got some information which he can put before the court as the beginnings of a case. On that view of the Judges' Rules the next question is: what information had the chief inspector when he started his interrogation which would have enabled him to put evidence before the court?


# Arrest Powers
Police have two arrest powers within the Crimes Act 1958.
- [[Section 458]] - Arrests relating to CAPS, summary offences or the escape of legal custody.
- [[Section 459]] - Arrests for indictable offences you **believe on reasonable grounds** has been committed.

Ultimately our attendance at a scene is to determine an arrest power (if any) in order to enforce the law. In order to best determine this, you must determine what offence (if any) has been committed. Doing so provides you with points of proof, giving you a framework to form a **belief on reasonable grounds** that a person committed that offence.

As put in **[R v Meade (Ruling No 1)](https://jade.io/article/304357) [2013] VSC 250 (‘Ruling 1’):**
>56. The term ‘suspect’ has a long-established meaning in the context of police questioning. As I have previously mentioned, the authorities make it clear that a suspicion that a person has committed an indictable offence **falls well short of a belief that they have done so**.

As put in **R v Dolan [1971]** by King CJ:
>It has always been accepted that while the investigation is at the stage at which the police officer is simply gathering information or giving possible suspects the opportunity of clearing themselves, there is no need for the caution… It seems to me, however, that where a police officer has reached a stage in his investigations at which he has reasonable ground for suspecting a particular person, he ought not to interrogate that person without advising him of his right not to answer questions.

As put in R v Osbourne [1972] by Ormiston J:
>My conclusion is that the word "suspect" requires a degree of satisfaction, not necessarily amounting to belief, but at least extending beyond speculation as to whether an event has occurred or not.

# Identifying an Offence
This point is self-explanatory. A vast majority of police officers understand how to identify offences. I would strongly recommend utilising the [Judicial College Charge Book](https://resources.judicialcollege.vic.edu.au/article/1053858) to assist in your identification if you're struggling.

# Evidence
Evidence comes in many forms, but typically takes the form of:
- Testimony (statements or oral evidence)
- Documents (photographs, receipts, emails, etc.)
- Physical (damage to property, injuries to a person)
- Forensic (fingerprints, DNA, blood)

## Testimony
The most common type of evidence is testimony. People provide a version of events to the court, or the police, outlining what occurred. In it's most basic form, **the victim statement**. 

In the circumstances where a victim provides a statement, remember that they have **sworn or affirmed** it is the truth. You must believe it on that basis. A statement without corroborative evidence, in my opinion, is enough to form a belief on reasonable grounds that a person has committed an offence. If it later comes to light that a false statement was provided, [Section 461(1) of the Crimes Act 1958 ](https://classic.austlii.edu.au/au/legis/vic/consol_act/ca195882/s461.html)(good faith argument) would protect any officer making this arrest.

In the circumstances where a victim **refuses** to provide a statement, what do you do? They provided an account, which was captured on body-worn camera, but it wasn't sworn or affirmed to. I would recommend:
- **Examine their credibility** - have you identified a lie? is there any other evidence to counter-act their story?
- **Consider why they won't sign a statement** - are they scared of the suspect? do you believe they would sign one later on?
- **What is the victim's motive** - Did they call 000 because they needed help or didn't know what to do? Or have they focused on the police doing one thing for them, and one thing only?

There is no clear guideline with this. Whether or not you believe a victim's unsworn / unaffirmed account is enough to form your belief is up to you. In my opinion, it is often not enough to rely upon alone.

The last thing to consider, is the admissions of a suspect himself. There often arises the case where you speak to a suspect who begins to implicate himself in a crime:
- You speak to a victim who provides an account, but refuses to sign a statement.
- You speak to a suspect without caution, who begins to make admissions to an offence.
- **Strongly recommend** you caution the suspect immediately, ask them to repeat / go back over what they admitted to.

I will cover below (in an example), what you would do if this were the case.

## Documents
Document evidence is often corroborative in nature. Text messages, emails, receipts, CCTV, photographs, etc. All of these assist in determining whether an offence has occurred.

Document evidence is difficult to alter or manipulate. Something like CCTV will provide a clear cut answer as to what happened. I would recommend considering:
- Is there any CCTV available? If it doesn't visually capture what occurred, does it capture the audio?
- Do text messages provide context to the offending? Has a party deleted a lot of text messages and can't explain that?
- If a photograph is said to exist, but a party denies it, check their recycling bin and ensure they haven't tried to delete it. 

## Physical 
Physical evidence is your best friend. It cannot lie. If it has been intentionally disturbed, that can be detected. If it hasn't, it tells a story from the past.
- What offence did you attend for?
- In damage offences:
	- What has been damaged?
	- How has it been damaged?
	- Who could have damaged it this way?
- In assault offences:
	- Is anyone injured?
	- What are their injuries?
	- Does the suspect have injuries too?
	- Do the suspects hands have marks, blood, bruises, etc. on them?
	- Is clothing ripped or disturbed on either party?

You **must** utilise physical evidence to your advantage. In my experience, it often tips the balance in favour of belief or suspicion. It can also assist in providing credibility to a victim refusing to sign a statement, but providing a narrative. If the physical evidence lines up, it suggests they're most likely telling the truth.

# Arresting a Person
If you've made it through the above information, you'll have a good feeling for whether or not you have a belief of a criminal offence occurring and the person having committed that offence. 

## Example: Criminal Damage
You attend an incident for criminal damage relating to family violence. There is a man and woman in relationship, with holes in walls and doors when you arrive.
- Both parties say the holes are accidents, someone fell over into the wall.
- Both parties refuse to provide a full account of what has occurred, neither will sign a statement or make admissions to an offence. 
- You have no physical evidence to support the crime, no ones hands look injured, no one is covered in plaster dust, etc.

At the time, you can't form a belief of reasonable grounds that any offenders exist. However, a day later, the female attends a police station and provides a statement to police saying the male punched holes in the walls to intimidate her. She didn't want to provide a statement on the night due to fears of retribution from her partner seeing her do so.

As a statement has now been provided, you have enough evidence to form a belief on reasonable grounds to arrest the male for Criminal Damage.

## Example: Stalking
You attend an incident which is initially put through as a dispute. There is a male and female who are ex-partners. The male isn't present at the time and they no longer reside together.

The female outlines that she is sick of the male and wants him out of her life. She says that five times in the last couple of months, he has appeared at her gym. She's sick of it. But she doesn't want him charged, and doesn't provide a statement.

Police attend the male's address to speak with him. Whilst in the information gathering stage (pre-caution), he implicates himself in an offence by saying he placed a tracking device on the female's vehicle. 

This example can go one of two ways;
1. The male is cautioned and repeats what he has said, making admissions to the tracking device being placed on her vehicle; arrest and interview as a result of admissions under caution.
2. The male is cautioned, but says no comment; do you have any evidence to form a belief?

This second branch is what leads a lot of police officers to strife. He is now saying no comment, but prior to this, he made non-cautioned admissions. Your evidence is inadmissible technically, but you've formed a belief due to his implications. 

This is clarified in Slaveski v State of Victoria & Ors 2010 VSC 441:
>101. The information upon which the police officer formed his or her belief may take a variety of forms and relate to a wide variety of matters. **When the reasonableness of that belief is in issue, the fact that the information may be inadmissible as hearsay at a later trial of the arrested person is irrelevant because it is put forward, not as evidence of its own truth, but as part of the material that is said to have given the police officer reasonable grounds for the belief.**

In summary: whether or not information is inadmissible (e.g. hearsay) at court is not relevant to their arrest. A police officer can still form belief on reasonable grounds based on information that is not admissible at court.
