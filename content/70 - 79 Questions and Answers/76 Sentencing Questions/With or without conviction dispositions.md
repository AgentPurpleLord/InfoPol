# Issue
What does a Magistrate need to consider when determining whether they will find someone guilty without a conviction?

# Rule
## Section 8 of the Sentencing Act
[[Section 8 Conviction or non-conviction]] provides the framework with regard to the discretion a court can use in determining conviction or non-conviction findings.

In summary, the nature of the offences, the character and past history of the offender, and the impact of recording a conviction on the offender's economic or social well-being, or on their employment prospects.

## R v Sessions [1998] 2 VR 304
Hayne JA, Batt JA, Eames AJA 
The accused had pled guilty in the County Court to 1x Rape and 1x Recklessly Causing Serious Injury. He was convicted and sentenced to three years' imprisonment on the rape and five years on the RCSI. The offence arose from an incident when the accused was changing the eight month old victim's nappy. He became annoyed with her crying and pushed his index finger once into her vagina, causing part of the intestine to be extruded through the vaginal opening.  
The accused sought leave to appeal against conviction for the RCSI and against the sentence for rape on the grounds that he was sentenced twice for the same Act. Essentially on the grounds of double jeopardy.

It was held by JA's Eames, Batt and Hayne that a conviction *should not have been recorded* for the RCSI, as the sentencing judge was obliged by s 5(2)(db) of the Sentencing Act 1991 to have regard to the serious injuries caused to the victim. 

This was also put on the basis that the common law principle was that a circumstance of aggravation may be taken into account as long as it is not the subject of a separate and more serious charge. In this case, the offence of rape is more serious that RCSI. 

## R v McFarling, Roderick Donald [1996] VicSC 555 (20 November 1996) (unreported)
- **Accused:** Roderick Donald McFarling, born 1955.
- **Charges:** 43 counts of theft under _Crimes Act 1958 (Vic), s 74_.
- **Trial outcome (1995):**
    - Directed _not guilty_ on counts 1–6, 8, 15–22, 32.
    - Jury found _not guilty_ on counts 7, 9–11, 13, 33–43.
    - Jury found _guilty_ on counts 12, 14, 23–31 (11 counts).
- **Sentence (County Court):** Convictions recorded; adjourned with 18 months good behaviour undertaking.
- The accused sought leave to appeal the matter in relation to both the *conviction* and *sentence* as being inconsistent with the directed not guilty and jury not guilty verdicts.
	- The accused argued insufficient weight was given to s 8(1) of the Sentencing Act 1991 as to the effect of a conviction on the accused.
- At the sentencing, it was submitted to the judge that a *without conviction disposition* would be appropriate due to the affect on future prospects of employment. 
	- HH reply to this was: "You have now been employed for some time as an accountant at Ragg Weir & Co. It was halfheartedly, I took it, suggested that conviction may affect your employment. However, I am reluctant to accept this bearing in mind that the employer, who I was told was aware of the trial, was not called to give evidence to this effect. I do accept that conviction would prevent registration with the Society of Accountants but as you are not fully qualified I do not consider that to be particularly relevant."
- In relation to *considering s 8(1)*, VSC found that the sentencing judge had failed to consider the impact of recording a conviction against the applicant's prospects of future employment. 

## DPP v Robinson [2000] VSCA 190
- The accused pleaded guilty to six counts of making a misleading written statement as a director contrary to s 85(1) of the Crimes Act 1958.
- The maximum penalty was seven years for that offence.
- The accused was discharged without conviction.
- The DPP launched an appeal arguing five grounds:
	- The sentence was manifestly inadequate; and
	- The sentence was disproportionate to the crime that it would shock the public conscience; and
	- The case might furnish the appellate Court with an opportunity to provide guidance concerning occasions when a sentencing judge or magistrate should or should not record a conviction.
- The sentencing judge provided the following remarks regarding conviction:
	- "Nevertheless, had they fallen for curial consideration shortly after their commission, I should think that a conviction of them ought to have been recorded. But, in fact, more than ten years have elapsed since their commission, and the passage of those years places the offences in a wider perspective than would have existed shortly after their commission.”


# Conclusion
Where the accused has no prior convictions and it can be demonstrated that a conviction recording would affect their prospects of employment, there is strong grounds for a without conviction disposition.
