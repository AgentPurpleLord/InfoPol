>[!info] [Section 10 of the Control of Weapon Act 1990](http://www8.austlii.edu.au/cgi-bin/viewdoc/au/legis/vic/consol_act/cowa1990216/s10.html "Section 10 of the Control of Weapon Act 1990")  - Primary Search Power
>(1) If-
>	(a) police has **SORG** person carrying or has in possession in a public place, a weapon contrary to this Act; and
>	(b) police inform person of grounds for SORG; and
>	(c) police comply with (3)-
>Police officer may w/o warrant-
>	(d) search **person**; and **any vehicle**; or **any thing in [[possession]]**; or **any thing under control for weapon**; and
>	(e) **seize** and **detain** item detected during search is SORG is a weapon

- You **must** inform the person of your name, rank and place of duty; and if requested provide that in writing.
- You **must** inform the person what Act and Section you are searching them under.
- You **must** conduct the least invasive search that is practicable.
- You can take into account an area of high violent crime to determine your SORG.

As discussed in [Murray, Hale and Olsen v The Queen (2017)](https://jade.io/article/546867 "Murray, Hale and Olsen v The Queen (2017)") how you determine your SORG is an extremely important topic. It must be formed from:

- A suspicion a normal person could have (e.g. large bulges in their clothes that outline the shape of a weapon).
- It cannot be tainted from your experiences as a police officer.
- It cannot solely be from having found them with a weapon in their hand (e.g. you must have another reason beyond having already located something in their [[possession]]).
- You must weigh the value of the search compared to depriving the liberties from the person in order to obtain a good criminal justice result.

# Goldberg v Brown (2003)
Another piece of Case Law from the UK [(Ghani v Jones [1970])](http://www.uniset.ca/other/cs3/19701QB693.html "(Ghani v Jones [1970])") was found to be applicable in Victoria [(Goldberg v Brown [2003])](https://jade.io/article/74958 "(Goldberg v Brown [2003])"). The Judge found that Lord Denning's Findings from Ghani v Jones were applicable in Victoria, specifically that the police must have reasonable grounds for believing:

1. A serious offence has occurred.
2. The article in question is 'fruit of the crime' (e.g. stolen goods) or an instrument used to commit the crime, or material evidence.
3. The person in [[possession]] of it has himself committed the crime, or is implicated in it.
4. The police must also not keep the article, or prevent its removal for any longer than reasonably necessary to complete their investigations or for evidence.
5. The lawfulness of the conduct of police must be judged **at the time**, and not by what happens afterwards.

The final point (5) is the most important. You **cannot** judge the lawfulness of a search **after** it has happened. Meaning if you begin an illegal search and locate illegal items, you do not have the grounds to continue your search. The whole thing will get thrown out at court if it is disputed.
