# Meaning of "towards"
In YY v ZZ [2013] VSC 743, the offender sought to find out the victim's address with assistance of another family member. Whether that behaviour constituted "towards" the victim per s 5(1)(a), Cavanough J stated at [66]:
>… for the purposes of the definition of “family violence” in s 5 of the Act …, conduct directed to finding out the address of a person can amount to behaviour “towards” that person that is “emotionally or psychologically abusive” or “threatening” or “in any other way controls or dominates” the person and causes the person “to feel fear for the safety or wellbeing” of the person. In my view, behaviour can be “towards” another person within the meaning of s 5 if it is focused on, or targeted at, or directed to, that person. To endeavour to find out a person's address is to engage in conduct that is focused on, or targeted at, or directed to, that person. It may be that, if the person does not become aware of the behaviour, the behaviour will not be “emotionally or psychologically abusive” within the meaning of s 5(1)(a)(ii) of the Act. It might not “control” or “dominate” within the meaning of s 5(1)(a)(vi) either. On the other hand, it might still be “threatening” behaviour within the meaning of s 5(1)(a)(iv). In the criminal law, an accused may be guilty of a “threat to kill” even if the proposed victim is not the listener to, or the recipient of, the threats. And it is noteworthy that s 74(3) of the Act envisages that family violence might occur even where the perpetrator and the affected family member are in different jurisdictions at the time. However, it is unnecessary to determine these questions. The fact is that [the victim] did become aware of the plaintiff's behaviour. His Honour has eloquently expressed his findings as to the impact that the plaintiff's behaviour had on [the victim] once she became aware of it. His Honour also found that the plaintiff would have been well aware of the impact that his behaviour would have on his daughter if she became aware of it. Obviously, there was always a risk that [the victim] would become aware of it. Accordingly, it was open to the judge in the circumstances of this case to characterise the conduct found to have been engaged in by the plaintiff as “family violence” against [the victim], within the meaning of and for the purposes of ss 5 and 74(1) of the Act.

# Meaning of "violence"
The meaning of "violence" was discussed in Re Swain [2017] VSC 55, the applicant was seeking bail regarding contravention charges to cause harm or fear because the applicant had pressured the AFM to support his application to gain access to firearms. The applicant had pressured the AFM to deny earlier allegations of threats made by him that he would use firearms against her. 
Defence tried to argue that in construing "violence", in the phrase "used or threatened to use violence" should mean specifically "family violence." That was rejected by the court, at [9]-[15]:
>Without attempting to consider all the differences between the plain meaning of the word “violence” and the statutory definition of “family violence”, it is sufficient to make two observations. The first is that the statutory definition of “family violence” is narrower than the plain meaning of the word “violence” in the sense that it is limited to behaviour by a person towards a family member of that person. The second is that, leaving aside the first observation, the behaviour defined as “family violence” is more expansive than the plain meaning of the word “violence”.

# Meaning of "threatening"
In Kirby v Phelan [2003] VSC 43 at [12] (case was using the Crimes (Family Violence) Act 1987 which is now repealed), Bongiorno J considered what amounted to a threat. 
The respondent had admitted to taking an axe intending to smash the AFM's car.
>The concept of a threat is not without its difficulties. In usual parlance it means something said by one person to another or something done by one person in the presence of another which conveys some notion of an intention to inflict harm of one kind or another. But there may be situations where the term could be appropriately applied to conduct not fitting that description. It may well be that there are circumstances where things done, even alone, could constitute a threat …
