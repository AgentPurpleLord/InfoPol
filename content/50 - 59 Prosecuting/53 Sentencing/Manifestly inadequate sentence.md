In [_R v ADJ_ (2005) 153 A Crim R 324](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U2&serNum=2006549274&pubNum=0005341&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&ppcid=10fe9aa38b4548b88114e130ace720e4&contextData=\(sc.Search\)) (Vic CA) Batt [JA](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U5&docFamilyGuid=Icc3925ebee7d11e8af46ddc380776190&pubNum=1100190&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&docVersion=Law+in+Force&ppcid=10fe9aa38b4548b88114e130ace720e4&contextData=\(sc.Search\)), with whom the others agreed, said (at 338 [51]):
> Whether a sentence is manifestly excessive (or manifestly inadequate) is a conclusion which does not admit of much elaboration. For a challenge to a sentence as being manifestly excessive or manifestly inadequate to succeed the sentence must be unreasonable or plainly unjust.

In [_Dinsdale v The Queen_ (2000) 202 CLR 321; 115 A Crim R 558; 175 ALR 315](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U2&serNum=2000502019&pubNum=0003586&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&ppcid=10fe9aa38b4548b88114e130ace720e4&contextData=\(sc.Search\)) Gleeson CJ and Hayne J said (at 325–326; 317–318; 561 [6]):
> Manifest inadequacy of sentence, like manifest excess, is a conclusion. A sentence is, or is not, unreasonable or plainly unjust; inadequacy or excess is, or is not, plainly apparent. It is a conclusion which does not depend upon attribution of identified specific error in the reasoning of the sentencing judge and which frequently does not admit of amplification except by stating the respect in which the sentence is inadequate or excessive. It may be inadequate or excessive because the wrong type of sentence has been imposed (for example, custodial rather than non-custodial) or because the sentence imposed is manifestly too long or too short. But to identify the type of error amounts to no more than a statement of the conclusion that has been reached. It is not a statement of reasons for arriving at the conclusion.

In [_DPP (Vic) v Raddino_ (2002) 128 A Crim R 437](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U2&serNum=2002315488&pubNum=0005341&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&ppcid=10fe9aa38b4548b88114e130ace720e4&contextData=\(sc.Search\)) (Vic CA) Chernov [JA](https://anzlaw.thomsonreuters.com/Link/Document/FullText?refType=U5&docFamilyGuid=Icc3925ebee7d11e8af46ddc380776190&pubNum=1100190&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&docVersion=Law+in+Force&ppcid=10fe9aa38b4548b88114e130ace720e4&contextData=\(sc.Search\)) with whom the others agreed said (at 444 [30]):
> This court has often said that a ground of manifest inadequacy does not admit of a deal of argument. 
> 
> Once the relevant circumstances are ascertained, then the sentence upon its face appears manifestly inadequate or it does not.

In R v Osenkowski (1982) 30 SASR 212, King CJ stated at [213]:
>... The proper role for prosecution appeals, in my view, is to enable the courts to establish and maintain adequate standards of punishment for crime, to enable idiosyncratic views of individual judges as to particular crimes or types of crime to be corrected, and occasionally to correct a sentence which is so disproportionate to the seriousness of the crime as to shock the public conscience.
