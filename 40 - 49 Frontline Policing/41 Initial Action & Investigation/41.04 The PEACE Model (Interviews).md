# The PEACE Model
Try not to think of the PEACE Model as a strict guideline or set of steps that you need to follow. It informs a process most Police Officers will naturally fall into and helps provide a step-by-step framework for you to follow during the course of your interviews with either suspects or witnesses. It stands for:

1. Prepare and Plan
2. Engage and Explain
3. Account
4. Closure
5. Evaluation

# Prepare and Plan
This very first step can potentially inform the outcome of your interview. It is worth taking the time to sit down and have a planning session on how to achieve the objectives you want to achieve during the interview.

Consider doing the following:

- Create and record an interview plan.
- Determine the characteristics of your interviewer; are they hostile or compliant? Are they of Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander descent? Do they have a disability?
- Practical arrangements; what room, who is going to sit where?

# Interview Plan
Most people won't make time for this because they think they know the offence after dealing with it for the last four hours. Make the time. A method of mapping out an interview plan is to create a grid (of however many squares you want). In each square, write a topic heading you want to cover off in the interview. That's your plan. Avoid writing out specific questions.

- Review your investigation.
- Establish what materials you have; CCTV, physical evidence, etc.
- Decide on your aims and objectives of the interview; are you trying to get admissions?

Some important questions to ask:

- Who needs to be interviewed and in what order?
- Why is a particular interviewee's viewpoint important?
- What information should you obtain?
- Should you interview the immediately, or is it better to wait until you gather more evidence?

# Engage and Explain
The beginning of your interview should be about building **rapport**, explaining the **process** and explaining the **expectations** of the interview to the person you're interviewing.

Explain the process the person is about to go through:

- I believe you have been involved.
- My role is...
- Explain note-taking and the DEC machine.
- Discuss their concerns and the processes.
- Address any breaks they may wish to take, or you may need to take.
- And explain what will happen at the end of the interview.

# Recorded Interview Commences
Your interview is broken down into three stages:

1. Suspect Agenda
2. Police Agenda
3. Challenge

Go through your 1313's Interview Preamble and lead into a 'planned initial open question'. The question is designed to encourage a free narrative response (the accused tells their story).

# Suspect Agenda
> "Tell me about your involvement in the theft of DVD's at Big W this morning in as much detail as possible?"

Consider that the above question may agitated the accused, due to you very directly accusing them of something they might maintain isn't true. Always plan for an alternative if you think the accused will be annoyed by your questions relating to the offence:

> Tell me your movements from first leaving the home this morning until the time you were arrested, in as much detail as possible.

At this stage you want to focus on open-ended questions to lock the accused into their story. Take and show a geniune interest in what they are talking about, not just the offence and it's points of proof.

# No Comment
When a suspect replies with 'No Comment', you might be thrown off balance. Your questions might not be getting the free narrative reponse you want and that can be frustrating. Don't fret and stick to your plan. **Move on from the suspect agenda.** Go straight into the Police Agenda.

**Most importantly, DO NOT ASK:**"Do you intend to answer 'no comment' to all of my questions?" or "What is your reason for answering 'no comment' to my questions?"

By asking this question, you will be making the remainder of your interview inadmissible.

You can read more about why in [Centre for Crime Investigation - Investigative Interview (July 2018, Version 1.1) (pp 35).](http://intranet/files/documents/142843_PEACE-Investigative-Interviewing-Booklet-Ver-1.1.pdf#search=IADVOKATE "Centre for Crime Investigation - Investigative Interviewing")

# Police Agenda
This stage of the interview is about what we **want** to know. The suspect agenda will provide you with non-contentious topics you may want to explore. Tie up defences, points of proof and any non-contenious exhibits you may have. Only explore topics they have presented to you thus far.

Non-contentious means items that will **not** cause an argument.

# Challenge Phase
Take a break from the interview so that you have an opportunity to gather your thoughts on how to proceed.

Your challenges go in order from least impactful to most impactful. They should all be concise and direct. They are contentious topics that will create either an argument or directly discount something the accused has said.

> "Earlier on your said that you were never at the Big W store today..."  
> "Is that correct?"  
> "I have CCTV footage that depicts a person matching your description inside the store..."  
> "How do you explain that?"

**Don't:** Read victim or witness statements out verbatim to the suspect.  
**Do:** Paraphrase or summarise the evidence you have.

# Realistic Interviews
Realistically, you're not going to be interviewing Victoria's next biggest murder case. The above process has it's place and works very well. But when you're interviewing someone for a $9.99 theft charge, you don't necesarily need to suspend the interview before the challenge phase. In those cases, my advice is:

- Follow the same structure of Suspect Agenda, Police Agenda, Challenge Phase.
- Don't worry too much about suspending the interview (if it's straight forward).
- Read the room. If your suspect is compliant and admitting what they've done, get out of the standard cycle questions and let it flow naturally.

# T.E.D.S
## Tell Me
- "Tell me everything about the incident (e.g. assault) yesterday..."
- “Tell me in as much detail as possible, your movements between [X time] and [X time] yesterday…”
- “Tell me everything you saw...”
- “Tell me more about that...”
- “Tell me what you mean by...”

## Explain
- “Explain what you saw”
- “Explain what happened”
- “Explain how you got ready on that morning”
- ”Explain to me how that happened”

## Describe
- “Describe the layout of the car park”
- “Describe the route you took”
- “Describe how he hit you”
- “Describe the person from top to toe”

## Show Me / Sketch
- “Show me what you mean”
- “Show me how he approached you”
- “Sketch your route to work”
- “Sketch the emblem you saw on the jumper ”

# Conversational Funnel
Taking witness statements relies on utilising a conversational funnel:

1. Free Narrative TEDS questions
2. Probing 5WH (Who, What, Where, When, How)
3. Fine Grain Detail

Through using all of these techniques we can "triage" the witness statement by **identifying** topics, **probing** those topics and **summarising** them into an effective witness statement.
